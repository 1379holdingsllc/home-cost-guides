How Much Does Pressure Washing Cost? 2026 Price Guide

Updated 2026-10-20 · Figures are typical estimates reported by the sources cited at the end of this page.

Pressure washing is one of the cheapest ways to make a house look years newer, but quotes can look wildly different from one company to the next. One crew says $180, another says $650, and both may be honest. This guide explains what published cost surveys report for 2026, why the numbers vary so much, and how to read a quote so you know what you are actually buying.

Quick answer

National cost surveys put a typical whole-house wash somewhere in the low-to-mid hundreds of dollars. Angi reports an average of about $312 for pressure washing a house, with most jobs between roughly $100 and $720 depending on size and location. HomeGuide lists house washing at about $170–$360, with a broader national average for all pressure washing jobs around $211. Per square foot, Angi cites $0.10–$0.50 depending on the surface. These are typical estimates from those sources, not fixed prices; your local market and your specific property will move the number.

Why quotes vary so much

1. What is being measured

Some companies price by the home's living-area square footage pulled from public records. Others measure the actual wall surface being cleaned, which on a two-story home can be much larger than the floor area. HomeGuide's guide for contractors specifically warns pros to tell customers which method they use. If one quote is per "house square foot" and another is per "surface square foot," they are not comparable until you convert them.

2. Siding material

Vinyl siding is the most common and usually the simplest to wash. Stucco, painted wood and older brick need lower pressure, more chemical dwell time, or hand work around mortar joints. Angi's 2026 data lists different per-square-foot bands for vinyl, stucco, brick and wood siding, and wood is generally at the higher end because it can be gouged by too much pressure.

3. Height and access

A single-story ranch with a clear perimeter is fast. A three-story home on a slope with landscaping against the foundation means ladders, extension wands and more time protecting plants. Height is one of the most common reasons a quote jumps.

4. How dirty it is

Light dust and pollen rinse off. Heavy mildew on the north side of the house, red clay splash along the bottom course, or rust and irrigation stains need pre-treatment. In humid Southern climates like metro Atlanta, organic growth on shaded walls is common, which is why many crews "soft wash" siding with a detergent mix rather than blasting it.

5. Minimum charge

Almost every company has a trip minimum. HomeGuide reports typical minimums of $150–$400. That is why a small patio alone can cost nearly as much as a driveway plus patio: you are paying for the truck, setup and travel either way. Bundling surfaces in one visit is the single easiest way to lower your per-square-foot cost.

Pressure washing vs. soft washing

Pressure washing uses high-pressure water, sometimes heated, to strip dirt off hard surfaces like concrete. Soft washing uses low pressure plus a cleaning solution that kills mildew and algae, then rinses it away. Concrete driveways and sidewalks usually get pressure; siding, roofs and painted surfaces usually get soft washed. A good quote will say which method is used on which surface. If a company plans to blast vinyl siding or asphalt shingles at full pressure, ask why — that can force water behind siding, damage window seals, or strip roof granules.

Common add-ons and what they do

  • Concrete sealing after a driveway wash protects against oil and staining. It is a separate line item and needs dry weather.
  • Gutter brightening removes the black "tiger stripes" on the outside face of gutters. It is not the same as cleaning debris out of the inside.
  • Rust or clay stain treatment uses specific chemicals; standard washing will not remove it.
  • Deck wash plus stain is a bigger project. Washing prepares the wood; staining is a separate trade step with its own cost.
  • Window rinsing after a house wash prevents spots but is not a full window cleaning.

DIY vs. hiring a pro

Consumer electric pressure washers are inexpensive and fine for patio furniture or a small slab. The risks show up on siding, wood and anything above the first floor: etched concrete from holding the tip too close, water forced into wall cavities, and ladder falls while holding a recoiling wand. If you only need a small patio done once, DIY can make sense. For a full house, a two-story wall, or anything involving chemicals for mildew, a professional with insurance is usually the better value.

How often should you pressure wash?

Most homeowners do siding once a year and concrete every one to two years. Shaded walls, homes near trees, and properties in humid climates tend to need it more often. Washing before painting, before listing a home for sale, or after a pollen season are common timing choices.

What to ask before you book

  1. Is the price based on the home's square footage or the surface area being cleaned?
  2. Which surfaces get high pressure and which get soft washed?
  3. What chemicals are used, and how are plants and pets protected?
  4. Is there a minimum charge, and what does it cover?
  5. Are you insured for property damage, and can you show proof?
  6. What happens if stains remain after the wash?
  7. Do you need water from my outdoor spigot?

How to lower the cost

  • Bundle house, driveway and walkways into one visit to beat the minimum charge once instead of twice.
  • Ask neighbors if they want to book the same day; some crews discount multiple homes on one street.
  • Book outside the spring rush when crews have open days.
  • Move cars, furniture and planters yourself before the crew arrives.

How to compare pressure washing quotes

Pressure washing bids are hard to compare because two crews can quote the "same" house while planning very different work. Before you look at price, line the bids up on these points:

  • Soft wash or high pressure, surface by surface. A good bid says which surfaces get low-pressure detergent (siding, painted trim) and which get surface-cleaner pressure (concrete). A bid that just says "pressure wash house" leaves that to chance.
  • How the size was measured. Living-area square footage and actual wall area are different numbers on a two-story home. Ask each company which one it used.
  • What's in the "house" price. Gutter faces, soffits, the front walk, the porch ceiling and window screens are often included by one company and extras for another.
  • Plant and runoff protection. Ask how they pre-wet and rinse landscaping, and where the rinse water goes.
  • Stain expectations in writing. Rust, irrigation, oil and red-clay staining often don't come out with a standard wash. A reputable crew tells you up front what probably won't come out.
  • Water source. Most crews use your outdoor spigot. Confirm that, and whether they need a specific hose connection.

A very low price paired with "high pressure everywhere" is the main thing to watch for. It is fast and cheap to do, and it's how siding, window seals and wood get damaged.

Notes for metro Atlanta homeowners

Atlanta houses get dirty in a predictable way: yellow pine and oak pollen in spring, green mildew on shaded north walls through the humid summer, and orange red-clay splash along the bottom of the siding and on the driveway. Watching the Atlanta Allergy & Asthma pollen count before you book is worth it. Washing in the middle of peak pollen season can mean a fresh yellow coat days later. Many homeowners schedule right after pollen drops off in late spring, then again in fall. Red-clay staining on concrete and light siding is iron-based and often needs a specific rust or clay remover rather than more pressure, so mention it when you ask for quotes. Ask whether the crew has a plan for keeping wash water from running into storm drains.

Frequently asked questions

Is pressure washing worth it before selling a house?

Often yes. A clean exterior photographs better and reduces buyer objections about mildew or stains. It is one of the lowest-cost curb appeal improvements.

Can pressure washing damage my house?

Yes, if too much pressure is used on siding, wood, window seals or shingles. That is why most siding and roofs are soft washed.
