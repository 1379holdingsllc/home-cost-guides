How Much Does Christmas Light Installation Cost? 2026 Price Guide

Updated 2026-10-18 · Figures are typical estimates reported by the sources cited at the end of this page.

Hanging holiday lights looks simple from the sidewalk. Up close it means ladders against gutters, clips on every few inches of roofline, extension cords run to outdoor outlets, and a second trip in January to take it all down. That is why professional Christmas light installation has become a regular seasonal service, and why quotes range from a couple hundred dollars to several thousand. The big variables are how many feet of lights you want, how high they go, and whether the installer is hanging your strands or supplying their own. This guide lays out the published 2026 price ranges, explains why the per-foot figures from different sources look so far apart, and covers removal, storage, power use and permanent lighting.

Quick answer

Angi reports a 2026 national average of about $444 for professional Christmas light installation, based on 2,242 projects from its pros, with most homeowners paying $220–$684 and most installers charging $2–$5 per linear foot. Thumbtack puts the national average at $260–$661, with a low end of about $133 and a high end of about $1,338. HomeGuide reports $250–$1,500 for a basic roofline package, $750–$5,000 for larger homes or lights on more features, and an average of $5–$12 per linear foot. All of these are typical estimates from those sources, not quotes.

Why the per-foot numbers don't match

Three published per-foot figures can look contradictory: $1.25–$3, $2–$5 and $5–$12. They are mostly measuring different things. Thumbtack's $1.25–$3 per linear foot is the labor fee when you already own the lights, and it adds $0.50–$2 per linear foot if the installer supplies the strands. Angi's $2–$5 is its typical installer rate, which climbs with steep roofs and difficult access. HomeGuide builds its installed-cost table on $5–$12 per foot and notes that some companies strictly rent out and use their own lighting, while others will hang your lights and lower the price accordingly. Before comparing two quotes, find out whether each one is labor only, labor plus rented lights, or labor plus lights you keep.

Thumbtack says most installers charge twice as much to hang lights on a second story as on the first, and that a hard-to-reach roof may add another 20 percent for special equipment.

How many feet of lights you need

Because nearly every installer prices by the foot, it helps to know your footage before you call. HomeGuide says a smaller home with a basic outline may need only 50–200 linear feet, a mid-sized home 100–300 feet, and full coverage of windows, garage and landscaping 400 feet or more. Thumbtack says most homes of 1,000–2,000 square feet need at least 10 to 20 strands. HomeGuide's table at $5–$12 per foot shows how fast the total grows:

A quick way to estimate: walk the front of the house and count the gutter runs and peaks you want outlined, add the width of the garage door frame and any windows, and write the total down. Even a rough number lets you check whether a per-foot quote adds up.

How installers charge for labor

Angi lists three common pricing models: a minimum fee of $150–$300 per job, a per-foot rate of $2–$5, or an hourly rate of $60–$100. The minimum fee matters most for small displays. If you only want the front gutter line done, you may pay the minimum no matter how short the run is. Angi says multi-story homes, steep roofs and bad weather all add labor time, and that rates vary by region and by the installer's experience. It also notes that prices in the Northeast and West tend to run higher than in the Midwest and South.

The lights themselves

If you buy lights, Thumbtack puts strands at about $4–$30 each depending on quality and type, and notes that LEDs cost more up front but use less energy and last longer. HomeGuide says commercial-grade LEDs cost more but are what most professionals use, and that rented lights usually come with removal built into the price. If a strand fails later, Angi lists replacing a string at $20–$50 and wiring repairs or reattaching loose sections at $50–$100 per hour. Angi's rule of thumb is that if repairs approach half the cost of a new installation, replacement is usually the better call, and that lights more than five years old are strong candidates for an upgrade.

Trees, wreaths and extras

Wrapping trees is priced separately from the roofline. Thumbtack reports $60 for a small tree up to $1,200 for a large one. A deciduous tree with open branches needs tight wrapping along each limb, while a cone-shaped conifer usually takes less time and fewer lights. HomeGuide lists window outlines, wreaths, tree wraps and inflatables as features that push the price up, and Angi adds timers, smart controls, color-changing lights, custom design and synchronized shows. Angi also notes that site prep, such as cleaning gutters or removing old lights, can add to the total.

Removal and storage

Takedown is the half of the job people forget to price. HomeGuide lists removal at $100–$400 when it isn't already in the package, and Thumbtack says removal typically costs about half of what you paid to have the lights put up. HomeGuide says most professionals recommend taking lights down by mid-January and that many installers return two to four weeks after New Year's Day. Angi says removal and storage are usually priced separately, but bundling installation with removal and storage can earn a discount, and storage protects the lights for next season.

Electricity and safety

Angi estimates a large incandescent display can add $30–$75 a month to an electric bill, while an equivalent LED setup typically adds $5–$20. HomeGuide says a medium LED display run six to eight hours a night costs just a few dollars for the whole season. Timers keep the lights from burning all night. On safety, the U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission advises never stringing together more than three sets of incandescent lights and never overloading electrical outlets. HomeGuide recommends checking for frayed wires and using outdoor-rated extension cords outside.

Permanent lights: worth it?

Permanent lighting mounted along the eaves is the main alternative to hanging and removing lights every year. HomeGuide prices it at $20–$40 per linear foot, or $2,000–$8,000 for 100–200 feet, and notes many systems change color, so they can be used for other holidays. As rough arithmetic using the sources' own rates, 150 feet of seasonal installation at Angi's $2–$5 per foot is $300–$750 a year, while 150 feet of permanent lighting at HomeGuide's rates is $3,000–$6,000 once. Whether that trade makes sense depends on how many years you plan to stay in the house and how much you value never climbing a ladder for lights again.

DIY or hire it out?

Angi says DIYers need lights, extension cords, mounting clips and possibly ladders or safety gear, and that a full display can take several hours or an entire weekend. The bigger issue is risk: falls, electrical problems and property damage, especially on multi-story homes and steep roofs. Angi advises working only in dry weather, using proper equipment such as an extension ladder, and never doing it alone. A one-story ranch with easy gutter access is a reasonable weekend project. A two-story front with steep gables is where most homeowners decide the fee is worth it.

When to book

Angi says demand peaks in late fall and early winter and that booking early is one of the most reliable ways to get better rates. HomeGuide recommends confirming the timeline for both installation and removal, especially in busy seasons.

How to compare Christmas light installation quotes

Holiday lighting quotes are hard to compare because each company bundles different things into one number. Ask every installer to answer the same questions in writing:

  • Whose lights are they? Labor only on your strands, lights rented for the season, or lights you buy and keep. Thumbtack and HomeGuide both treat these as different prices, so make sure every bid is the same kind.
  • Exactly what gets lit, and how many feet. Roofline only, or roofline plus windows, garage, walkway and shrubs. Ask for the measured footage per area so you can check the math.
  • Minimum charge. Angi says many installers have a job minimum. A small display may cost the minimum no matter how short it is.
  • Removal date and storage. Is takedown included, and on what date? If they store rented lights, is storage part of the price?
  • Bulb-out service. HomeGuide notes some installers bundle mid-season maintenance. Ask how fast they come back if a section goes dark the week before a party.
  • How lights attach. Clips on gutters and shingles, or anything that leaves holes in fascia or trim. Ask what they use and how removal is handled.
  • Power and timers. Which outlets they'll use, how many runs per circuit, and whether a timer is included. The CPSC advises against overloading outlets.
  • Warranty. Angi says some installers cover workmanship and bulb replacement and others don't. Get it in writing.

A price that looks far below the published ranges usually means labor only on your own lights, roofline only, or removal billed later. Those can all be fine choices, but compare them against bids that include the same things.

Notes for metro Atlanta homeowners

Metro Atlanta's mild early winters make it tempting to hang lights later, but they also change how the season plays out. Most of the installing happens in pleasant weather, so the main hazards here are rain and wind rather than snow. Wet shingles and ladders on damp clay soil are a bad combination, so expect a reputable crew to reschedule on rainy days, and check the National Weather Service Peachtree City office forecast before your install and takedown dates. January can still bring a freeze or an ice storm, which is one more reason to get removal on the calendar in writing. Late fall is also when Atlanta's oaks and pines fill gutters, and clips on a gutter packed with leaves and needles don't hold well. Angi notes gutter cleaning can be part of site prep, so schedule the gutter cleaning before the lights go up. Many metro Atlanta homes are two stories with steep front gables, which is where Thumbtack's second-story and hard-to-reach surcharges apply, so mention the height when you request quotes. Many neighborhoods also have HOA rules, and Angi recommends checking with your city or HOA before you schedule. Finally, plan for the tree. The City of Atlanta Office of Solid Waste Services lists discarded Christmas trees as yard trimmings for curbside collection, and the city runs a Christmas tree recycling program, so City of Atlanta residents should check those options before paying a hauler to take away a real tree. If you live outside the city limits, check your county or hauler's rules.

Frequently asked questions

When should I book Christmas light installation?

Earlier is better. Angi says demand peaks in late fall and early winter and that booking early is one of the most reliable ways to get better rates. HomeGuide suggests confirming both the install date and the takedown date when you book.

Is removal included in the price?

Sometimes. HomeGuide says removal runs $100 to $400 when it isn't part of the package, and that companies renting you their lights usually include removal. Thumbtack says removal typically costs about half of what installation did. Ask before you sign.

How much will the lights add to my electric bill?

Angi estimates a large incandescent display adds $30 to $75 a month, compared with $5 to $20 for an equivalent LED setup. HomeGuide says a medium LED display run 6 to 8 hours a night costs only a few dollars for the season. A timer keeps it from running all night.

Are these prices guaranteed?

No. All figures on this page are typical ranges reported by the sources listed below, presented as estimates. Your actual price depends on your property, your location and the provider you choose.
