How Much Does Air Duct Cleaning Cost? 2026 Price Guide

Updated 2026-10-20 · Figures are typical estimates reported by the sources cited at the end of this page.

Air duct cleaning sits in an awkward spot among home services. Ads promise cleaner air and lower energy bills, while the U.S. EPA says cleaning is not something every home needs on a schedule. The honest middle: when ducts are visibly contaminated, pests have nested in them, or mold is growing on hard surfaces inside the system, cleaning can make sense. When the only issue is ordinary household dust, the benefit is less clear and a lowball phone special is more likely to be a bait-and-switch. This guide covers the published 2026 price ranges, what a proper job includes, and how to tell whether you actually need one.

Quick answer

Angi reports a 2026 national average of about $389 for air duct cleaning, with most homeowners paying $271–$509. It prices the work at roughly $0.15–$0.30 per square foot of home size, or about $25–$50 per vent (around $35 as a midpoint). HomeGuide reports a higher typical band of $450–$1,000 for a whole-house clean, citing the EPA and NADCA for that range, and prices supply vents at $25–$50 each and return vents at $40–$75 each, or about $0.20–$0.40 per square foot. Jobs with video inspection, dryer-vent add-ons or heavy contamination can push higher. These are typical estimates from those sources, not quotes.

When cleaning is actually warranted

The EPA duct-cleaning guidance is the best starting point. EPA does not recommend routine duct cleaning for every home. It says you should consider cleaning if there is substantial visible mold growth on hard-surface ducts or HVAC components, if pests or vermin have been in the system, or if ducts are clogged with debris that is blowing out of the registers. Light dust alone is normal and, by itself, is not a reason to hire a crew. If insulated duct lining is wet or moldy, EPA notes it generally cannot be cleaned effectively and should be removed and replaced rather than scrubbed. Fix the moisture source first, or mold will return.

Angi and HomeGuide both echo practical signs that line up with that guidance: musty smell from the vents, visible mold or pests near registers, dust blowing out when the system starts, filters clogging faster than they should, and allergy symptoms that track with HVAC use. None of those signs is a substitute for looking inside the system. Ask any company quoting you to show you what they see before and after, preferably with photos or a camera.

What a proper job includes

A legitimate whole-system clean covers more than wiping a few supply registers. Expect:

  1. Supply and return ducts. HomeGuide notes both should be cleaned. Returns often carry more debris because that is where air enters the system.
  2. Registers and grilles. Removed, cleaned and put back.
  3. The air handler / blower area. Dirt on the blower and nearby components can recontaminate freshly cleaned ducts if it is left alone.
  4. Negative pressure and agitation. Pros use a vacuum that puts the duct system under negative pressure while brushes, air whips or compressed air loosen debris so it is pulled out rather than blown into living spaces.
  5. Access and sealing. Technicians may cut temporary access openings in sheet-metal trunks and then seal them. Ask how they seal those openings when they finish.

Angi puts labor at about $90–$125 per hour and most jobs at 3–8 hours; HomeGuide cites a similar hourly band and roughly 2–4 hours for a two-person crew on a typical whole-system clean. Flex duct and hard-to-reach runs take longer than straight metal trunks.

What drives the price up or down

  • Home size and vent count. More square footage and more registers mean more labor. Count your vents before you call so phone quotes are based on the same number.
  • Duct type. Rigid metal is usually easier. Flexible duct needs gentler tools and can cost more, per both Angi and HomeGuide.
  • Access. Crawlspaces, attics, tight soffits and homes without floor vents add time. Atlanta-area homes on crawlspaces and with attic air handlers are common examples.
  • Contamination level. Heavy dust, construction debris after a renovation, smoking residue, or lots of pet hair push a job past a standard package price.
  • Mold, pests or asbestos. Angi lists pest removal around $200–$1,200, mold remediation about $1,100–$3,400, and asbestos work at roughly $5–$20 per square foot that must be handled before cleaning continues. HomeGuide's mold and asbestos bands for related work run higher still. Those are separate specialties, not a $99 duct special.

Be careful with chemical “sanitizing” sprays sold as add-ons. EPA's guidance is cautious about unnecessary biocides in ductwork. Ask what chemical would be used, why it is needed, and whether it is registered for that use. If there is no mold or odor problem, you may not need it.

Red flags and the $99 special

Air duct cleaning is one of the most scam-prone home services. Classic warning signs:

  • A whole-house price that is far below the Angi and HomeGuide ranges (for example, under about $100) for a multi-vent home.
  • High-pressure door-to-door or phone pitches that invent health claims the EPA does not support.
  • Refusal to show you the inside of the ducts before or after, or using a shop-vac on a few registers and calling it done.
  • Upsells sprung after the crew is inside (“your ducts are the worst we've seen”) without photos or a clear written change order.
  • No insurance, no local address, and no willingness to put scope and price in writing.

NADCA membership or similar trade credentials are not a guarantee, but a company that can explain its method, show equipment, and price a whole-system clean in the published bands is usually safer than one competing only on a teaser price.

DIY vs. pro

Homeowners can clean register covers, vacuum what they can reach at openings, and keep filters changed. That is useful maintenance, not a substitute for negative-pressure whole-system cleaning. The tools pros use (truck-mounted or large portable vacuums, rotary brushes, air whips) are not practical to rent for a one-off, and aggressive DIY brushing can damage flex duct or knock debris into the living space. Hire out when EPA's conditions are met; otherwise put the money into filter changes, sealing obvious leaks, and fixing moisture problems.

How often?

HomeGuide cites every 3–5 years as a common industry recommendation depending on system age. That is a rule of thumb from the trade, not an EPA mandate. EPA's position remains as-needed. Households with renovations, heavy remodeling dust, smokers, or multiple pets may need attention sooner; others may go longer without a full clean. Changing filters on schedule does more for day-to-day air quality than an annual duct cleaning you do not need.

How to compare air duct cleaning quotes

Air duct cleaning quotes are easy to game with vague scope. Line bids up on:

  • Whole system or registers only. Confirm supply ducts, return ducts, registers and the blower/air-handler area are all included. Wiping a few grilles is not a duct cleaning.
  • Vent count and method. Give every company the same vent count. Ask whether they use negative-pressure vacuum plus agitation (brushes or air whips), and what they do with flex duct.
  • Before-and-after proof. Ask for photos or camera video of the main trunk and a few runs. Refuse to pay for mysteries.
  • Add-ons in writing. Video inspection, dryer vent, sanitizer, coating and furnace cleaning should be priced up front, not sprung after they open a wall.
  • Access openings. If they cut into sheet metal, ask how those openings are sealed when the job is done.
  • Credentials and insurance. Local business identity, liability insurance, and a written scope beat a door-hanger special.

Walk away from any bid that leans on medical claims the EPA does not make, or that cannot explain why your system needs cleaning beyond "everyone should do it yearly."

Notes for metro Atlanta homeowners

Metro Atlanta's long pollen seasons and humid summers are why duct cleaning gets pitched so hard here. Pollen and dust do load filters faster in spring (watch the Atlanta Allergy & Asthma pollen count), but a dirty filter is fixed by changing the filter, not automatically by hiring a duct crew. Humidity and condensate issues are the bigger local concern: poorly sloped or insulated ducts in hot attics can sweat, and wet insulation is a mold risk the EPA says usually needs replacement, not scrubbing. Many Atlanta-area homes put the air handler in the attic or crawlspace, which affects access pricing, so mention that when you request quotes. After spring pollen drops, and again before heavy winter heating, are sensible times to inspect registers and filters; clean ducts only if inspection shows a real problem. Same-day "$99 whole house" offers that flood neighborhood Facebook groups deserve the same skepticism here as anywhere else.

Frequently asked questions

Does the EPA say I should clean my ducts every year?

No. The EPA does not recommend routine duct cleaning. It suggests considering cleaning when there is substantial visible mold on hard-surface ducts, pests in the system, or debris clogging ducts and blowing into living spaces.

Are $99 whole-house duct specials legitimate?

Treat them with skepticism. Published national ranges from Angi and HomeGuide for a typical whole-house clean are much higher. Ultra-low teaser prices are a common bait-and-switch pattern in this trade.

Are these prices guaranteed?

No. All figures on this page are typical ranges reported by the sources listed below, presented as estimates. Your actual price depends on your property, your location and the provider you choose.
