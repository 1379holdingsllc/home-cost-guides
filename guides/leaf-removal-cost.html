How Much Does Leaf Removal Cost? 2026 Price Guide

Updated 2026-10-18 · Figures are typical estimates reported by the sources cited at the end of this page.

Fall leaf cleanup is one of those jobs that is easy to underestimate until the yard is buried. A couple of shade trees can drop several cleanups' worth of leaves between October and December, and wet leaves matted on a lawn are heavy, slow work. Professional leaf removal prices depend mostly on how big the yard is, how many trees shed onto it, how the crew collects the leaves, and where the leaves go afterward. This guide pulls together the published 2026 ranges, explains why per-visit prices and per-season contracts look so different, and covers the cheaper alternative of mulching leaves back into the lawn.

Quick answer

Angi reports a 2026 national average of about $379 for leaf removal, based on 35,524 projects from its pros, with most homeowners paying $191–$592, or about $0.02–$0.10 per square foot and $400–$1,000 per acre for larger lots. HomeGuide reports $150–$700 for most residential lawns and $450–$1,000 per acre, with large or heavily treed lots reaching $1,000 or more. Lawn Love, using its own completed jobs from the past 12 months, reports an average of about $172 per visit, with most homeowners spending $79–$326. All of these are typical estimates from those sources, not quotes.

Raking, blowing or vacuuming

How the crew collects leaves changes both the hourly rate and how long the job takes. Angi and Lawn Love publish nearly identical hourly ranges:

Raking$25–$50$25–$50$150–$450
Blowing$15–$45$15–$45$150–$600
Vacuuming$40–$60$37–$60$200–$700

The cheapest hourly rate isn't always the cheapest job. Lawn Love notes that vacuuming costs more per hour than raking but clears large yards fast, which often makes it cheaper overall than paying a crew to rake all afternoon. HomeGuide says commercial vacuums shred leaves as they collect them, reducing volume dramatically, and recommends raking for small yards, tight spaces and areas where blower noise is a concern. Angi suggests blowing as the way to keep labor costs down if you mainly want the lawn clear, with raking around delicate plants and mulched beds.

Disposal is a separate line

Collecting leaves is half the job. Getting rid of them is the other half. Angi says labor makes up about 90–95 percent of a leaf removal bill, roughly $170–$570 for most homeowners, with the rest going to fuel and contractor bags. If the pro hauls the leaves away, Angi adds $5–$10 per bag in dumping fees, and lists curbside leaf pickup at up to $100. HomeGuide describes the main options as bagging for curbside pickup or landfill, mulching in place, or composting on site. On a yard that fills dozens of bags, per-bag fees add up quickly, so ask how each quote handles disposal.

Lawn Love makes the same point: more visits cost more per year but less per visit. Angi says committing to an annual contract often earns a discount.

How often leaves need to come up

Angi recommends removing leaves at least twice per fall. A yard with a tree or two can usually get by with two cleanups, a yard with many trees needs at least monthly service through the fall, and weekly visits keep a yard looking tidy. HomeGuide says the best time for a big cleanup is late fall, after most leaves have dropped, and that heavily treed properties may need several visits. Angi explains the reason not to skip it entirely: heavy, matted leaves can compress grass, hold too much moisture and block sunlight.

Trees matter as much as acreage

HomeGuide says mature oaks, maples and other deciduous trees produce far more debris than evergreens, that large leaves such as sycamore are easier to collect than small birch leaves, and that pine needles need different removal techniques, which can change the price. If your lot has a mix of hardwoods and pines, tell the company so it can plan for both.

Common add-ons

Many companies sell leaf removal alongside other fall work. Angi lists gutter cleaning at $1–$2 per linear foot, garden bed cleanup at $60–$100, lawn aeration at $75–$200 and mulching at $50–$150. HomeGuide lists gutter cleaning at $100–$250, aeration at $100–$300 and dethatching at $100–$300. Combining the gutter cleaning with the final leaf visit is efficient because the gutters fill with the same leaves.

Comparing booking platforms

Lawn Love publishes a comparison of typical per-visit leaf removal prices across hiring platforms: $79–$326 on Lawn Love, $145–$175 on GreenPal, $174–$487 on Lawn Guru, $131–$374 through Thumbtack or Angi, and about $170 on LawnStarter. Keep in mind that Lawn Love is one of the companies being compared, and that none of these platform prices replace a quote for your specific yard.

DIY: rake, blow or mulch

Leaf cleanup is a very doable DIY job if you have the time. Lawn Love puts a rake at about $25, a leaf blower at about $150 and a leaf vacuum at about $150. Angi suggests raking when leaves are dry, since wet leaves are heavier, and raking onto a tarp you can drag to the curb or compost pile. Angi estimates the average homeowner saves about $379 by doing the work.

The cheapest option of all is often not removing the leaves. A University of Georgia Extension article describes mulching leaves with an ordinary mower: discharge toward the center of the lawn, run over the windrow repeatedly until most pieces are dime-size or smaller, then spread them back out. It says a mulching blade isn't required, but a blade with worn fan tips mulches poorly, and that wet, matted leaves won't pick up. With a little rain, the shredded leaves break down in a few days and return nutrients and organic matter to the soil. Angi likewise notes that a small amount of leaves left to decompose can feed the soil, while Lawn Love warns that a heavy layer left whole will smother grass. The practical middle ground is to mulch light leaf cover with the mower and pay for removal only when the volume is more than the lawn can absorb.

Should you tip?

HomeGuide says tipping isn't required, but homeowners who do usually give 10–20 percent of the total or $10–$20 per crew member, and some tip once at the end of the season rather than after every visit.

How to compare leaf removal quotes

Two leaf removal quotes can cover very different amounts of work. Before comparing totals, give every company the same scope and ask:

  • Which areas are included? Lawn only, or also beds, under shrubs, the driveway, patio, deck and the strip along the street. Walk the property and list them.
  • Where do the leaves go? Hauled away, bagged and left at the curb, mulched into the lawn, or piled at the back of the lot. Hauling and bag fees (Angi cites $5–$10 per bag) should be on the quote, not a surprise.
  • Method near beds. Blowers move mulch and pine straw along with leaves. Ask how they handle beds and delicate plants.
  • Minimum fee. HomeGuide suggests asking whether there's a minimum, which matters for small yards and touch-up visits.
  • Mulching option. HomeGuide also suggests asking whether they charge extra to mulch the leaves instead of bagging them. Mulching can reduce disposal costs.
  • Per visit or per season. Get both prices. A two- or three-visit fall package is easier to compare against a one-time cleanup when you know the per-visit equivalent.
  • Gutters and roof. Is clearing gutters included, priced as an add-on, or not offered?
  • Late leaf drop. If a big oak drops after the final visit, will they come back, and at what price?

A very low price often means the crew blows leaves to the curb or the woods line and leaves disposal to you. That can be a fair deal if your city collects bagged leaves for free, but know which one you're buying.

Notes for metro Atlanta homeowners

Angi's state figures put Georgia's average leaf removal cost at about $340, a little below its national average of $379. The bigger local issue is what happens to the leaves. Inside the City of Atlanta, the ATL311 yard trimmings guidance says leaves go out for curbside collection in brown paper yard trimming bags of no more than 35 pounds each, up to 20 bags per collection, or in a reusable container no larger than 32 gallons marked YARD TRIMMINGS on four sides. Plastic bags aren't accepted, city garbage and recycling carts shouldn't be used for yard trimmings, and 21 or more bags must be scheduled as a bulk collection. ATL311's Missed Yard Trimmings page says yard trimmings are collected every other week, so time your cleanup to your collection week rather than leaving bags at the curb for days. City of Atlanta residents (not businesses) can also drop yard debris at Hartsfield Landfill on James Jackson Parkway. One rule matters a lot when you hire help: ATL311 cites City code Sec. 130-40(c)(4), which makes contractors responsible for removing the yard debris they generate. That means a crew that rakes your yard and leaves 40 bags at the curb may be leaving you with a problem, so confirm that haul-away is in the price. Cities and counties outside Atlanta, including in Cobb, DeKalb, Gwinnett and Henry, set their own rules, so check with your hauler before bagging. Many metro yards mix hardwoods with pines, and pine straw beds are common, which is why the method near beds matters. Finally, the UGA Cooperative Extension mower-mulching approach works well on lawns with light leaf cover and can cut down on bags.

Frequently asked questions

How many leaf cleanups do I need each fall?

Angi recommends at least two per fall for a yard with a tree or two, and at least monthly through the fall for yards with many trees. HomeGuide says a big cleanup works best in late fall after most leaves have dropped.

Can I just mow the leaves instead of removing them?

Often, yes, if the leaf layer is light. A University of Georgia Extension article describes mulching dry leaves with a mower until the pieces are dime-size or smaller, then spreading them out to break down. Lawn Love warns that a heavy layer of whole leaves left on the lawn will smother the grass.

Does the price include hauling the leaves away?

Not always. Angi says haul-away often adds $5 to $10 per bag in dumping fees. In the City of Atlanta, ATL311 says contractors are responsible for removing the yard debris they generate, so confirm haul-away is included before the crew starts.
