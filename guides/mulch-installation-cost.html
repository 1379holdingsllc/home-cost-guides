How Much Does Mulch Installation Cost? 2026 Price Guide

Updated 2026-10-20 · Figures are typical estimates reported by the sources cited at the end of this page.

Fresh mulch is one of the cheapest ways to make a yard look finished. It also does real work: it holds moisture in the soil, keeps weeds down and evens out soil temperature. Mulch is sold and spread by the cubic yard, so pricing is easier to compare than many outdoor jobs, but only once you know how many yards your beds need and what the crew is including. This guide gathers the published 2026 price ranges, shows how to estimate quantity yourself, and covers the add-ons and installation mistakes that change the result.

Quick answer

Angi reports a 2026 national average of $120 for professional mulch delivery and installation, with most homeowners spending $75–$325 for a standard yard. Angi says small jobs can cost as little as $50, while larger projects can exceed $800. HomeGuide puts installed mulch at $50–$150 per cubic yard, or $25–$100 per cubic yard for the material alone, and $2–$8 per bag. Fixr reports a national range of $150–$400, with most homeowners spending about $275 for 3 cubic yards of bark mulch delivered and installed. Fixr's low end is about $80 for 1 cubic yard of straw raked out, and its high end about $700 for 5 cubic yards of cedar mulch blown in.

Figure out how much mulch you need first

Every quote is really "number of yards × price per yard," so measuring your beds is the single most useful thing you can do before calling anyone. Measure the length and width of each bed, multiply to get square feet, and add the beds together. Then use this formula:

Cubic yards = square feet × depth in inches ÷ 324

The 324 comes from the 27 cubic feet in a cubic yard times 12 inches in a foot. HomeGuide says one cubic yard covers about 108 square feet at 3 inches deep, and Fixr uses a rounder 100 square feet. For depth, HomeGuide suggests 2–3 inches on beds that were mulched before and 3–4 inches on new beds. As an example, 500 square feet of beds at 3 inches takes about 4.6 cubic yards, while a 1-inch top-up on the same beds takes about 1.5.

If you're buying bags, HomeGuide says most hold 1 to 3 cubic feet, so it takes 9 to 27 bags to equal one cubic yard. Fixr bases its math on a 2-cubic-foot bag, or about 13 bags per yard.

Labor: by the yard, by the hour or blown in

Angi says installers charge $20–$50 per cubic yard or $35–$65 per hour, and some charge a flat fee. HomeGuide's labor range is $25–$50 per cubic yard. Fixr quotes $55–$75 per hour for a two-person crew and says 3 cubic yards takes about two hours, or roughly $110–$150 in labor. On big jobs some companies blow mulch through a hose instead of wheelbarrowing it. Fixr puts mulch blowing at about $35–$60 per cubic yard, or $105–$180 for 3 yards, comparable to hand spreading. Angi says blowing speeds the job up but can add to the cost.

Delivery is sometimes separate. HomeGuide lists delivery fees of $50–$150 depending on distance and order size. Beds behind the house, on slopes or far from the driveway take more trips, and both Angi and HomeGuide say limited access or sloped yards can raise labor costs.

Color matters too. Fixr lists brown and red mulch at $30–$40 per yard and black at $40–$60, and Angi adds $10–$30 per cubic yard for specialty mulch such as dyed, rubber or organic blends. HomeGuide notes that darker colors absorb more heat and may break down faster. If you choose dyed mulch, Fixr recommends looking for the Mulch and Soil Council (MSC) certification, which indicates the product is free of chromated copper arsenate. Fixr also warns that cocoa hull mulch can be poisonous to dogs.

Texture affects where a mulch works best. Fixr says light bark pieces migrate more and suit flat beds, that pine bark can wash away where water runs off, and that double-shredded hardwood holds up better on slopes. That's worth knowing before you pay for a cheaper mulch that ends up in the street after the first big storm.

Angi says add-ons like edging, weed fabric and old-mulch removal together can add $150–$300 to a typical project. Angi also notes that permits are rarely needed for home mulch jobs, though work near wetlands or in protected areas can carry fees of $25–$100.

Top-off or full replacement?

Most beds don't need the old mulch pulled out. Angi prices a top-off at $30–$60 per cubic yard and a full replacement with removal at $50–$110 per cubic yard, and says replacement makes sense when the old layer is heavily decomposed, moldy or full of pests, or when you're switching materials. Its rule of thumb: if refreshing would cost close to half of a complete replacement, replace. Fixr agrees that replacement is rarely necessary and is mostly done to change color or material. For upkeep, Angi suggests adding one to two inches each season and replenishing annually or every other year, and says professional bed maintenance runs about $100–$300 per year.

Don't pay for a mulch volcano

More mulch isn't better. A University of Georgia article warns against the cone-shaped piles often seen around landscape trees, which it calls mulch volcanoes. A UGA Extension horticulturist quoted there says piling mulch around a plant's base sets it up for long-term stress. Deep piles can starve roots of oxygen, keep the lower trunk constantly moist and invite canker diseases, and give termites and rodents a place to live. The article says a 3-inch layer after settling is enough for most plants. It recommends spreading mulch out to the drip line, the edge of the canopy, and pulling it a few inches back from the trunk. It adds that a 6-inch layer of bark may restrict gas exchange in the soil and keep it too wet during rainy periods. Fixr similarly recommends 2–3 inches of organic mulch kept about 6–12 inches from the base of woody plants. If a crew's quote is based on heaping new mulch over old every year, you may be paying for a problem.

DIY vs. hiring a crew

Spreading mulch takes only a wheelbarrow, a rake, a shovel and gloves, so it's one of the most DIY-friendly yard jobs. Angi estimates about an hour per cubic yard for spreading and cleanup, and says doing it yourself saves $20–$50 per cubic yard in labor. Fixr points out that bagged and bulk mulch cost about the same for 1 or 2 yards, with convenience the main difference, while larger amounts are usually cheaper in bulk. Hiring out makes the most sense for big orders, steep beds or bad backs. How to compare mulch installation quotes

Mulch quotes are easy to compare once everyone is quoting the same volume of the same product. Ask each company:

  • How many cubic yards, at what depth? Get both numbers. A low total that assumes 1 inch isn't the same job as one that assumes 3.
  • Price per yard, installed. Divide the total by the yards quoted so you can line bids up.
  • Which mulch? Pine bark nuggets, double-shredded hardwood, dyed or natural? If it's dyed, ask whether it carries Mulch and Soil Council certification.
  • What prep is included? Weeding, re-cutting bed edges, raking out matted old mulch, or a pre-emergent weed preventer? HomeGuide suggests asking about edging and pre-emergent costs up front.
  • Old mulch. Are they topping off over it or removing it, and if removing, is haul-off included?
  • Delivery and minimums. Is delivery in the per-yard price, and is there a minimum charge for small jobs?
  • Hand-spread or blown? Either is fine, but you should know which you're paying for.
  • Trunks, stems and siding. Will they keep mulch pulled back from tree trunks and plant stems, and out of the strip right against the house? (See the Atlanta notes below.)
  • Cleanup. Are driveways, walks and patios blown off before they leave?

A sketch of your beds with rough measurements is usually enough for a firm price without a site visit.

Notes for metro Atlanta homeowners

Georgia's own landscape guidance favors simple, local mulches. The UGA Georgia Green Landscape Stewards mulching guide lists leaves as natural, free and nutrient-rich, and pine straw as cheap or free and sustainable. It notes pine bark comes in various sizes, decomposes slowly and can float or wash away. It marks hay as not recommended because it often contains weed seeds and herbicide residue, cypress because some is harvested from wetlands, and rubber because it adds no nutrients and may leach toxins. That last point is worth knowing because HomeGuide lists cypress and rubber among termite-resistant choices. UGA's checklist calls for a ring 2 to 3 inches deep out to a tree's drip line, with the root flare left exposed. Over tree roots that stick up through the lawn, UGA Extension in Forsyth County recommends a 3 to 4 inch layer instead of mowing over them, and says the organic matter relieves soil compaction and improves water infiltration over time. Water rules are the other local factor. Under the Georgia Water Stewardship Act, the Georgia EPD outdoor water use schedule allows outdoor landscape watering only between 4 p.m. and 10 a.m. for customers of EPD-permitted water systems. New plantings can be watered at any time for 30 days after installation, as can drip or soaker-hose irrigation and hand watering with an automatic-shutoff hose or a handheld container. Mulch that holds moisture in the soil makes those hours go further. Keep mulch out of the first 5 feet around the house, since the NFPA home ignition zone guidance recommends moving mulch, leaves and needles away from exterior walls. In the City of Atlanta, the ATL311 yard trimmings page says contractors are responsible for removing yard debris generated from landscaping work, citing city code Sec. 130-40(c)(4), so ask who hauls off old mulch and weeds. Cities and counties outside Atlanta set their own rules.

Frequently asked questions

When is the cheapest time to have mulch installed?

Off-season. HomeGuide says prices for yard work are often lower in winter, when demand is low, and higher in spring and summer. Fixr says you may find lower mulching quotes in fall and winter because landscaping is seasonal.

Does wood mulch attract termites?

HomeGuide says some wood mulches can attract and shelter termites. It suggests using thin layers instead of high piles, keeping beds well drained and watching for mud tubes. Separately, NFPA fire-safety guidance recommends keeping mulch away from the home's exterior walls.

How often should mulch be refreshed?

Angi suggests replenishing annually or every other year, typically adding one to two inches each season, and says organic mulches like bark and wood chips need attention more often than slower-breaking materials. Check the depth before topping off so beds don't slowly build up too thick.
