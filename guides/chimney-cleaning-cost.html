How Much Does Chimney Cleaning Cost? 2026 Price Guide

Updated 2026-10-18 · Figures are typical estimates reported by the sources cited at the end of this page.

A chimney is one of the few parts of a house that can quietly become dangerous without ever looking different from the street. Every wood fire leaves soot and creosote on the flue walls, birds and squirrels move in over the summer, and rain works on mortar and crowns year after year. A chimney sweep removes the fuel for a chimney fire and, just as important, looks for the cracks, gaps and blockages that let smoke or carbon monoxide back into the house. Prices vary more than people expect because a "sweep" can mean a 45-minute brush-and-look on a gas insert or a long afternoon on a neglected two-flue masonry stack. This guide lays out the 2026 ranges published by Angi, HomeGuide and Thumbtack and explains which kind of job you probably have.

Quick answer

Angi reports that a professional chimney sweep costs $129–$381 for an annual cleaning, with the average homeowner spending about $254. HomeGuide puts the average range at $150–$375 with a national average of about $275, a low of roughly $80 and a high near $700. Thumbtack reports a wider national range of $201–$726, with most people paying around $385, observed jobs from about $82 to $1,942, and hourly chimney cleaning and repair work averaging around $125 per hour. The spread between sources reflects what each one counts: Thumbtack's figures include more jobs where repair or heavy cleaning was bundled in. All of these are typical estimates, not quotes.

Why a gas fireplace costs less than a wood fireplace

The fuel decides most of the bill. Wood smoke condenses on the cooler upper flue as soot and creosote, a tar-like residue that can harden into a glaze. HomeGuide notes gas burns cleaner with less residue, which is why its gas range starts around $80. That does not make gas chimneys maintenance-free. The Chimney Safety Institute of America (CSIA) says gas-heated homes should still have every chimney inspected annually, because nests and debris can block a flue and older chimneys are not always sized correctly for modern furnaces and water heaters. If your gas logs share a chimney with a furnace or water heater flue, mention it when you call, since each flue is separate work.

Factory-built (prefab) fireplaces sit in the middle. They use a metal flue inside a framed chase, so there is no brick to scrub, but CSIA notes the deposits in these systems are acidic and can corrode components, so they should be swept whenever appreciable buildup appears. Wood and pellet stoves add a stove body, baffles and connector pipe to the job, which is why both sources price them above a plain gas fireplace.

Flues, height and access

Each flue is cleaned separately. HomeGuide prices one flue at about $100–$250, two flues at $250–$400 and three at $400–$700. Older homes with a fireplace on two floors, or a furnace flue beside the fireplace flue, often have more than one even if only one is used. HomeGuide recommends having every flue checked, including idle ones.

Access is the other big variable. Angi and HomeGuide both list steep or high roofs as a reason prices rise, because the sweep needs more time, more equipment and more fall protection. Thumbtack suggests telling every company the chimney's height above the roof, the roof's height off the ground, the roof pitch at the chimney, and what the chimney is built from, so each one quotes the same job. A one-story ranch with an easy-to-reach chimney is the job behind the low end of every published range.

Inspection levels and what they cost

Chimney inspections follow three levels defined in National Fire Protection Association (NFPA) Standard 211. Most annual visits are a Level 1, and most published sweep prices include one. The jump to Level 2 or 3 is where bills surprise people, so it helps to know what triggers each.

The Level 3 figures differ sharply because HomeGuide's band includes standard repair work for up to three flues, while Angi's covers the inspection itself. If a sweep recommends moving up a level, ask for the photos or video that prompted it and a written reason. Buying a house with a fireplace is the most common sensible reason to pay for a Level 2 up front.

Creosote, glaze and why neglected chimneys cost more

CSIA recommends sweeping a masonry fireplace once there is about 1/8 inch of sooty buildup, and sooner if any glazed creosote is present, because that is enough fuel for a chimney fire that can damage the chimney or spread to the house. Angi uses the same 1/8-inch rule of thumb. Loose soot brushes out quickly. Glazed, tar-like creosote does not, and CSIA describes sweeps using chemical treatments or rotary tools to break it down first, which adds visits and labor. That is the main reason Angi and HomeGuide both say a chimney that has gone years without cleaning costs more, and why HomeGuide says such a job can take twice as long as the usual one to two hours.

The cheapest way to keep a sweep visit at the low end is what you burn. Angi recommends only dry, seasoned hardwood, noting softwoods and unseasoned wood build creosote faster. EPA's Burn Wise guidance (now an archived EPA page) says to season wood for at least six months, burn it below about 20 percent moisture, and never burn trash, cardboard, painted or pressure-treated wood. Leftover deck boards and fence pickets do not belong in a fireplace.

Signs you need a sweep before the annual visit

  • Smoke spilling into the room, or fires that are hard to start and keep lit.
  • A campfire or tar smell from the fireplace, especially on humid days with the AC running. CSIA attributes this to creosote deposits and air being pulled down the flue.
  • Black flakes, crumbled mortar or tile pieces falling into the firebox.
  • Scratching, chirping or nesting material in the flue.
  • Water stains on the ceiling or wall near the chimney, or rusted damper parts.

Thumbtack also notes some owners schedule a cleaning before listing a home, since creosote odor can work against an otherwise good showing.

Can you sweep your own chimney?

Brushes and rods are sold at hardware stores, and the sources do not pretend it is impossible. The case against it is the inspection, not the brushing. HomeGuide warns that damaging a liner while cleaning increases the risk of fire and carbon monoxide, and CSIA says the chemical "sweeping logs" sold in stores are not a substitute for mechanical cleaning and inspection, because loosened debris still collects on smoke shelves and offsets. A reasonable split is to do the prep yourself (clear the hearth, remove ashes into a metal container, note anything unusual) and pay a certified sweep for the cleaning and Level 1 inspection.

Timing the appointment

Angi and HomeGuide both say spring and summer are the slow season, with shorter waits and sometimes lower rates, and that late summer and fall are the rush. HomeGuide adds that some sweeps offer off-season discounts for customers on an annual maintenance plan. Booking right after your last fire of the season also means any repair the sweep finds can be done before you need the fireplace again.

How to compare chimney cleaning quotes

Two chimney quotes can share a price and describe completely different visits. Before you choose, get each company to answer the same questions:

  • Is the inspection included, and which level? A sweep should come with at least a Level 1 inspection under NFPA 211. If a company can't tell you what a Level 1 is, keep looking.
  • Whole flue or firebox only? A complete sweeping covers the flue and smoke chamber, not just brushing the firebox bricks. Ask whether they work from the roof, the hearth or both.
  • How many flues are priced? Furnace and water-heater flues, and a second fireplace upstairs, are separate work. Make sure every bid counts the same number.
  • Photos or video. Ask for pictures of the flue interior and crown, especially if the visit ends with a repair recommendation.
  • Credentials. HomeGuide and Thumbtack both suggest asking about CSIA certification. Check that the certification is current.
  • Repairs quoted separately. A cap, damper or liner should be its own line item with a written price, not something added to the bill after the crew leaves the roof.
  • Drop cloths and cleanup. Ask how they protect the hearth, floor and furniture from soot, and whether they use a HEPA vacuum.

Watch out for rock-bottom chimney sweep phone offers that turn into a high-pressure liner pitch once the tech is inside. A cleaning priced far below the published ranges usually makes its money back on repairs. Get a second opinion with video before you sign for a liner or a rebuild.

Notes for metro Atlanta homeowners

Metro Atlanta chimneys deal with a wrinkle that northern guides rarely mention: chimney swifts. These small migratory birds nest in household chimneys across Georgia. The U.S. Fish & Wildlife Service notes they are protected by the Migratory Bird Treaty Act, which makes it illegal to harm the birds, their eggs or their nests. The Georgia DNR Wildlife Resources Division recommends having chimneys professionally cleaned between November and early March, after fall migration and before the swifts return, and keeping the damper closed during the March–August nesting season. If you hear chirping in the flue in summer, a reputable sweep will reschedule rather than clear an active nest. Practically, the swift calendar lines up with Angi's and HomeGuide's advice to avoid the fall rush: book soon after your last winter fire, or in late fall. Atlanta's humid summers also explain the musty, campfire smell many fireplaces give off in July, which CSIA attributes to creosote plus air being pulled down the flue. A sweeping and a tight-sealing top damper or cap usually help more than air fresheners do. Frequent summer thunderstorms are hard on crowns and flashing (check the National Weather Service Peachtree City office for severe weather), so ask your sweep to look at the crown and cap after a stormy season. Burn only seasoned wood, too. Freshly cut pine from storm cleanup is the kind of unseasoned softwood Angi says builds creosote faster.

Frequently asked questions

How often should a chimney be cleaned?

CSIA follows NFPA Standard 211, which calls for chimneys, fireplaces and vents to be inspected at least once a year and cleaned when needed. CSIA recommends sweeping a masonry fireplace at about 1/8 inch of soot, or sooner if glazed creosote is present.

Does a gas fireplace need a chimney sweep?

It needs an annual inspection even though it needs cleaning less often. CSIA notes nests and debris can block a gas flue. Angi and HomeGuide both price gas fireplace service lower than wood-burning, starting around $80.

How long does a chimney sweep take?

Angi says a standard sweep takes about 45 minutes to an hour. HomeGuide says 1 to 2 hours on average, and longer for chimneys that haven't been cleaned in years or need damage checked.

Are these prices guaranteed?

No. All figures on this page are typical ranges reported by the sources listed below, presented as estimates. Your actual price depends on your property, your location and the provider you choose.
