How Much Does Fence Staining Cost? 2026 Price Guide

Updated 2026-10-17 · Figures are typical estimates reported by the sources cited at the end of this page.

A wood fence is a long, skinny piece of exterior carpentry that sits in full sun and rain on both faces, with its bottom edge in wet grass. Stain is what keeps the boards from going gray, cupping and rotting at the base. Fence staining quotes swing widely because fences do: a 4-foot picket fence along the front walk and a 6-foot board-on-board privacy fence around the whole backyard are very different amounts of wood, even if both are "about 150 feet." This guide pulls together the published ranges, explains how pros measure and price the work, and covers the timing questions that decide whether a stain job lasts.

Quick answer

Angi puts the 2026 national average to stain a fence at about $1,900, with most homeowners spending $1,400–$2,200, and a per-foot range of $4–$14 per linear foot. Angi notes its figures are for one side of the fence, and that staining both sides adds about 80%. HomeGuide reports $2–$14 per linear foot, $0.50–$2.50 per square foot, or $25–$110 per 8-foot panel, with a typical total of $300–$2,800 for a 100- to 150-foot fence and most homeowners spending about $1,700. Thumbtack, which groups painting and staining together, reports a national range of $496–$1,432, with most people paying around $842. All of these are typical estimates from those sources, not quotes.

How to measure your fence before you call

Every source above prices by the linear foot, so the first number to have ready is the total length. Walk the fence with a tape or a measuring wheel, or use the length from your survey or the original fence invoice. Then note the height. HomeGuide's square-footage method is simple: multiply length by height for one side, multiply by 1.5 for shadow-box fences, and double it if both sides will be stained. A 150-foot, 6-foot-tall fence is about 900 square feet per side. Shadow-box styling pushes that to about 1,350, and both sides of a shadow-box fence come to about 2,700. That's why two neighbors with the same length of fence can get very different quotes.

By style, HomeGuide lists wood picket fences under 4 feet at $2–$5 per linear foot and wood privacy fences over 5 feet at $3–$10. Shadow-box (board-on-board) fences run $4–$14 because the overlapping boards create about 50% more surface to coat. Angi says split-rail and flat wooden fences with sparse vertical boards are the cheapest to stain, and lattice and other decorative work cost the most because of the extra prep and brush time. HomeGuide also prices metal, vinyl and chain-link painting, but those are paint jobs with rust primers or special coatings, not staining.

Prep: cleaning, repairs and the old finish

Stain has to soak into clean, dry wood. Prep is where cheap and careful bids part ways.

  • Washing. HomeGuide prices pressure washing alone at $1.50–$3 per linear foot and power washing plus staining together at $3.50–$17 per linear foot, or about $530–$2,600 for a 150-foot fence. HomeGuide recommends low pressure or a bleach-and-TSP cleaner. Thumbtack warns that too much pressure can damage a weak fence and says some pros prefer a cleaning solution to a pressure washer for that reason.
  • Repairs first. HomeGuide lists fence repair at $15–$35 per linear foot or $300–$900 on average, with small holes and cracks at $100–$300 and a leaning section at $250–$1,000. Staining a rotten picket only hides it for a season. Thumbtack suggests asking whether the stain contractor repairs fences or whether you'll need a separate fence company.
  • Old paint or solid stain. Thumbtack notes that if a painted fence is going to be stained, the paint has to come off first, while a fence that's already stained can usually just get a fresh coat. Removing a film finish is labor-heavy, so it should show up as its own line on the quote.
  • Vegetation. HomeGuide lists mowing and trimming back plants along the fence line as an accessibility cost. Clearing vines and weeds yourself before the crew arrives is an easy saving.

Gates and other add-ons

Gates take more time per square foot than straight fence. HomeGuide prices a walk gate at $100–$600 and a driveway gate at $200–$1,000. Thumbtack explains why: gates are handled constantly, often need an extra coat, and have to be brushed carefully around hinges, latches and the concrete or pavers under them. If your quote lists the fence at a per-foot rate, confirm whether gates are included or priced separately.

Stain vs. paint on a wood fence

HomeGuide lists fence stain at $20–$50 per gallon, covering about 200–300 square feet per coat, and exterior paint at $30–$80 per gallon, covering about 250–400 square feet. Paint needs a primer and two coats. Stain needs no primer and sometimes only one coat, but it soaks in, so it uses more product. Thumbtack says the upfront cost of staining can be higher for that reason, but painted fences need more upkeep later because paint can chip and peel. HomeGuide's pros-and-cons table says stain fades gradually and is less work to redo, which is the main reason most wood fences get stain rather than paint. HomeGuide notes stain comes in solid, semi-transparent and transparent finishes. Thumbtack says there is no single best fence stain because wood species absorb stain differently, and that oil- and water-based stains vary in opacity, UV protection and water protection, so ask your pro what they recommend for your boards and why.

DIY vs. hiring a crew

HomeGuide puts DIY materials at $200–$650, depending on fence size and condition. At 100 feet, for example, HomeGuide lists DIY at $130–$430 against $200–$1,400 for a professional. Angi's materials list includes stain at $20–$50, a sprayer at $60–$250, a belt sander at $55–$90 and sealant at $13–$40. If you rent equipment, HomeGuide lists a pressure washer at $80–$180 per day and a sprayer or power roller at $30–$50 per day. What you're really trading is time. Angi says a large fence can take a week or more for a homeowner, while a pro crew typically needs one to three days, and HomeGuide puts the full job, including cleaning and drying time, at 3–5 days. Angi adds that an airless sprayer can be five to ten times faster than a roller but takes practice, and Angi's top money-saving tip is doing the prep yourself even if you hire out the staining. Pro labor runs $50–$110 per hour (Angi) or $1–$9 per linear foot (HomeGuide).

Timing: new wood, weather and dry time

Most failed fence stain jobs come down to timing, not product.

  • New fences. HomeGuide says to stain a new fence 1–6 months after installation: cedar often after 1–2 months, and some pressure-treated wood after 3–4 months or longer, because it's still wet when installed. Thumbtack says roughly two months. Both use the same water test: sprinkle a few drops on the boards. If the water beads, the wood isn't ready. Waiting too long has its own cost, since HomeGuide says over-weathered gray wood needs sanding first.
  • Weather window. HomeGuide recommends 50°–90°F, low-to-average humidity, and a dry forecast for two or more days. Thumbtack says to give wood about 24 hours to dry after rain, or about 48 hours in cool or humid weather. Both name fall as the best season.
  • How much stain. HomeGuide recommends two coats for most fences but says to apply only as much as the wood will absorb. Too much stain leaves a film that traps moisture and peels.

Angi says a well-done stain job should hold up for a few years. A once-a-year look at the boards and an occasional low-pressure rinse is all the upkeep it suggests.

How to compare fence staining quotes

Fence staining bids are only comparable when every contractor prices the same fence. Before you compare totals, line the bids up on:

  • One side or both? This is the most common reason two quotes look far apart. Angi's ranges are for one side, and staining both sides adds about 80%. Ask every bidder to price both options.
  • Linear feet and height. Give every company the same measured length and fence height, and say whether any runs are shadow-box (board-on-board).
  • Gates counted separately? Get walk gates and driveway gates listed as their own line items.
  • Cleaning method. Low-pressure wash, chemical cleaner, or both, and how long they'll let the wood dry before staining.
  • Repairs. Loose pickets, leaning posts and rotted rails should be fixed or priced before staining starts, not discovered halfway through.
  • Product and coats. Brand, product line, opacity (transparent, semi-transparent or solid) and number of coats. Ask for the product name so you can read its label and data sheet.
  • Overspray protection. Sprayers save time but drift. Ask how they protect your house siding, cars, the neighbor's side of the fence, plants and hardscape.
  • Weather and rain policy. What happens if it rains within a day of application? Will they come back and fix it?

A bid far below the published per-foot ranges usually means the contractor is skipping the cleaning, staining only one side, or putting on one thin coat. That's not necessarily wrong, but you should know which before you sign.

Notes for metro Atlanta homeowners

Metro Atlanta weather narrows the staining window more than the national guides suggest. HomeGuide's target of 50°–90°F with low-to-average humidity and two dry days ahead rules out many summer afternoons, when heat and pop-up thunderstorms are common. Check the hourly forecast from the National Weather Service Peachtree City office and plan around storms, not just the daily rain chance. Spring has its own problem: the heavy yellow pine pollen that coats cars and decks also sticks to wet stain and dulls the finish, so watch the Atlanta Allergy & Asthma pollen count and wait until the peak passes, then rinse the fence again right before staining. That leaves fall, which HomeGuide and Thumbtack both name as the best season, as the practical choice for most Atlanta-area homeowners. Shaded fence lines under trees stay damp longer and grow mildew. Clean those sections thoroughly, give them extra drying time, and use the water-bead test on the shady side as well as the sunny side. Red clay splashes onto the bottom boards in every hard rain. Clean it off before staining, and keep mulch and soil pulled back from the bottom of the boards so the base can dry out. Many subdivisions around Atlanta have HOA covenants with architectural review, so if you plan to change the fence color, check your HOA rules and get any required approval before the crew arrives. For questions about wood decay and moisture on the property, the UGA Cooperative Extension publishes homeowner guidance for Georgia conditions.

Frequently asked questions

How soon can I stain a new fence?

Usually not right away. HomeGuide says to wait 1 to 6 months depending on the wood: cedar often after 1 to 2 months, and some pressure-treated lumber after 3 to 4 months or more. Thumbtack suggests about two months. If water beads on the boards, the wood isn't dry enough yet.

How long does fence staining take?

Angi says a pro crew usually needs one to three days. HomeGuide puts the full project at 3 to 5 days including cleaning and drying time, and longer in humid weather.

Should I stain one side or both sides?

Staining both sides protects the boards evenly, but Angi says it adds about 80% to the one-side price. If the back side faces a neighbor, check with them, and with your HOA if you have one, before the crew goes onto their side.

Are these prices guaranteed?

No. All figures on this page are typical ranges reported by the sources listed below, presented as estimates. Your actual price depends on your property, your location and the provider you choose.
