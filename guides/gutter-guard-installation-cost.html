How Much Does Gutter Guard Installation Cost? 2026 Price Guide

Updated 2026-10-20 · Figures are typical estimates reported by the sources cited at the end of this page.

Gutter guards are sold two very different ways. At one end is a box of foam inserts or plastic screens from a home center that you snap in yourself on a Saturday. At the other is a branded system pitched at your kitchen table, custom-fitted, backed by a lifetime warranty and quoted at several thousand dollars. Both are called "gutter guards," which is why the published prices for this job look like they describe different planets. This guide lines up the ranges from four major cost surveys, explains why they disagree, and shows what actually moves a quote so you can tell which kind of bid you're holding.

Quick answer

Angi reports a 2026 national average of $1,521 for gutter guard installation, based on 3,206 verified projects, with most homeowners spending $653–$2,457. Angi puts the installed price at $6–$13 per linear foot, materials and labor included. Thumbtack reports a similar national range of $622–$2,053, with most people paying around $1,179, though it says its figures are for 50 linear feet of guards.

HomeGuide is far higher: $3,000–$9,000 for 200 linear feet with professional installation, a national average of $6,000, or $15–$45 per linear foot. Its reported minimum is $600 and its maximum $14,000. For DIY, HomeGuide lists $1–$5 per linear foot, or $200–$1,000 for the same 200 feet. Fixr, in a guide last updated January 2025, reports $69–$600 with a $335 average, but that figure covers materials only for 150 feet of metal mesh guards; labor is billed on top.

Why the sources disagree so much

Three things explain most of the gap, and they're worth understanding before you compare bids.

  • What's being counted. Fixr's headline number is material only. Angi's and Thumbtack's ranges include installation. HomeGuide's $6,000 average is a professionally installed system, which it says typically includes high-quality materials and a lengthy warranty.
  • How many feet. Thumbtack prices 50 feet, Fixr 150 and HomeGuide 200. HomeGuide says most homes have gutter runs of about one-tenth the home's square footage, and Thumbtack says standard-size homes average 200 linear feet. A 50-foot price and a 200-foot price aren't comparable without converting.
  • Which product. A plastic screen and a branded micro-mesh system both count as "gutter guards." Per-foot prices for the cheapest and most expensive guard types differ several-fold in every source, so the mix of products behind each survey drives its average.

The practical takeaway: always convert a quote to a price per linear foot, and find out what product it's for, before deciding whether it's high or low.

Because guards are priced by the foot of gutter, not by the size of the house, the total scales almost directly with your gutter length. Angi's 2026 figures:

Each type solves a different debris problem:

  • Screens and mesh keep out leaves and larger debris. Angi notes that because standard mesh isn't very fine, small debris and grit can still get through. HomeGuide says plastic screens don't last long or hold up well in extreme weather, and recommends powder-coated steel so the screen doesn't rust.
  • Micro-mesh adds a very fine layer. Angi says it stops almost all debris, including pine needles and grit, and averages about $9 per foot installed. HomeGuide cautions that the smaller holes also make it more susceptible to clogging on the surface.
  • Foam and brush inserts sit inside the gutter. They're the most DIY-friendly, but HomeGuide says small debris such as pine needles and seeds collects in brush bristles, and that foam can grow mold and fungus or even sprout seedlings if it isn't checked.
  • Reverse curve (surface tension) covers, often called gutter helmets, cover the whole gutter and let water curl around a rounded edge while debris slides off. Angi says they're more involved to install because they attach to the roof fascia, and Thumbtack warns that some installs require replacing the gutters or moving shingles.

Lifespans differ just as much. HomeGuide lists 2–5 years for foam, 3–6 for plastic screen, 5–10+ for steel screen, and 10–20+ for perforated aluminum and surface tension covers. Fixr is more generous on foam, at up to 10 years. Thumbtack says aluminum or stainless steel guards can last 20 years or more with maintenance, while foam, brush and plastic may last only up to 5. A cheap guard that has to be replaced twice can end up costing more than a durable one bought once.

Materials vs. labor

Angi puts guard materials at about $1–$9 per linear foot and labor at $2–$6 per foot, though elsewhere in the same guide it says hiring a pro typically costs about $9 per foot in labor, give or take $2. HomeGuide's labor range is wider, at $3–$20+ per foot depending on guard type and job complexity. Fixr prices labor by the hour: $82–$149 per hour, averaging $114, at roughly an hour per ten feet of gutter. On Fixr's numbers, 150 feet of metal mesh comes to $69–$600 in material plus about $1,778 in labor on average, and its high-end tier runs $3,070–$3,670. The lesson is that on most professional jobs, labor and the installer's overhead are a bigger share of the bill than the guard itself.

Prep work that can add to the bill

Guards should go on clean, sound gutters. Angi recommends budgeting $120–$230 for a cleaning and $200–$650 for repairs before installation, and says full gutter replacement averages about $1,150. HomeGuide's figures for the same extras are cleaning at $100–$250, gutter repairs at $100–$450, soffit and fascia repairs at $500–$2,600, new gutters at $2,200–$5,000 for 150 linear feet, and tree trimming at $400–$900 per tree if branches hang over the roof. If an installer finds rotten fascia, it has to be fixed first, since that's what most guards and hangers fasten to.

Other things that move the price

  • Height and roof shape. Angi says multi-story homes cost more. Fixr notes a three-story house may need extension ladders, scaffolding and extra safety gear, and HomeGuide adds that steep pitches and complex rooflines take more material and time.
  • How the guard attaches. Angi says good guards are securely fastened rather than clipped on, and that less secure attachment is cheaper but less effective.
  • Where you live. Angi reports installed prices of about $4–$16 per foot depending on the market, from $6–$16 in New York to $4–$7 in Des Moines.
  • Season. Angi and HomeGuide both say spring and fall are the busiest seasons for gutter work. Angi suggests winter or summer installs may save money.
  • Gutter size. HomeGuide says most gutters are 5 or 6 inches wide, and guards have to match.

Do gutter guards end gutter cleaning?

No, and a contractor who promises that is overselling. Angi says experts recommend cleaning gutters at least once every two years even with guards or hoods installed, and HomeGuide says most experts recommend cleaning at least annually. What guards change is how often and how hard the job is. Debris that lands on many guard types is meant to slide, wash or blow off, but whatever slips through still builds up over time, and Thumbtack notes that debris can stick to reverse-curve covers in heavy rain until it's brushed off or dries. HomeGuide also flags that customer reports suggest some reverse-curve and fine-mesh guards don't perform as well in heavy rain, when fast-moving water can shoot past them. If you're comparing systems, ask installers for local references.

Are they worth it?

Angi says guards are worth it when tall trees surround the house and your gutters fill often, and estimates they can save roughly $120–$500 per year in cleanings. As a rough check using Angi's own figures, its $1,521 average install would pay for itself in about 3 years at $500 a year in avoided cleanings, or about 13 years at $120 a year. That's our arithmetic, not a survey figure, and it ignores the safety value of not climbing a ladder. If budget is tight, Angi suggests putting guards only on the runs most prone to clogging, such as the sections under trees.

DIY vs. hiring a pro

HomeGuide says brush, foam and screen guards are the most DIY-friendly and reverse-curve systems typically need a professional. Fixr prices a budget DIY job at $51–$86 for 100 feet of PVC covers, at least $125 for brush guards and about $147 at minimum for foam on the same length. The tradeoff is ladder work along every eave. Angi notes that DIY installation can affect gutter alignment and may invalidate a gutter warranty. If you mostly need clean gutters rather than new hardware, compare against our gutter cleaning cost guide, since one or two cleanings a year may cost less than guards on a small house with few trees.

How to compare gutter guard installation quotes

Gutter guard bids are hard to compare because two companies can quote completely different products for the same house. Put every installer on the same footing with these questions:

  • How many linear feet? Get the measured footage on every bid and divide the total by it. A price per foot is the only fair comparison.
  • What product, exactly? Brand, model and type (screen, micro-mesh, brush, foam or reverse curve), plus the mesh material. Stainless steel, aluminum and plastic perform and last very differently.
  • Do you clean, re-pitch and repair the gutters first? Ask whether cleaning, hanger tightening and sealing leaky seams are included or extra, and get a price for any fascia repair before work starts.
  • How is it attached? Screwed to the gutter lip or fascia, or slid under the shingles? Ask if anything touches the roof, and check your shingle warranty before agreeing to anything that does.
  • Downspouts and corners. Are inside and outside corners, valleys and downspout openings covered, or only the straight runs?
  • What does the warranty actually cover? Clog-free performance, materials, labor, or all three? Is it transferable if you sell, and does it require paid annual cleanings to stay valid?
  • Who does the install? Angi's reviewing roofer recommends picking a company with its own in-house crew rather than subcontractors.
  • Is that price good past today? Be wary of a "today only" discount. A real quote should hold long enough to get a second opinion.

If one bid is a branded system and another is a local installer's micro-mesh, compare price per foot and warranty terms side by side rather than headline totals.

Notes for metro Atlanta homeowners

Pine trees are the main reason Atlanta-area homeowners look at gutter guards. UGA Extension says pine straw falls year-round but needle-fall is heaviest in fall, winter and early spring, so gutters under pines keep refilling well after the hardwood leaves are gone. Needles matter when choosing a guard type. Angi says micro-mesh is designed to block fine debris like pine needles, while HomeGuide warns that pine needles and seeds can collect in brush guards, and that plastic screens let smaller particles through. If your roof sits under pines, ask installers specifically how their product handles needles and get references from nearby homes with similar trees. Fire safety is another reason to keep gutters clear. The NFPA home ignition zone guidance recommends cleaning roofs and gutters of dead leaves, debris and pine needles that could catch embers. Heavy rain is the other consideration. HomeGuide says larger gutters are best for areas with frequent heavy rain, and that some reverse-curve and fine-mesh guards may not perform as well in downpours, so ask how a system handles heavy rain before you buy. Spring and fall are the busiest seasons for gutter work, according to Angi and HomeGuide, so booking an install in a slower month may get you a better date and, per Angi, possibly a better price.

Frequently asked questions

Will gutter guards void my roof warranty?

They can. HomeGuide lists a possibly voided roof warranty among the downsides of gutter guards, and Thumbtack says some reverse-curve installs involve moving roof shingles, which may invalidate a roof warranty. Ask whether the product touches the shingles and check your warranty before signing.

How long does gutter guard installation take?

Thumbtack says the installation itself can take several hours, after an estimate visit to measure. Fixr's labor estimate assumes roughly an hour per 10 feet of gutter, so time on site, and the labor bill, grows with gutter length and roof height.

Can I add guards to only part of my gutters?

Yes. Angi suggests installing guards only on the sections most prone to clogging, such as runs under trees, if budget is a concern. Make sure the quote spells out exactly which runs are covered.

Are these prices guaranteed?

No. All figures on this page are typical ranges reported by the sources listed below, presented as estimates. Your actual price depends on your property, your location and the provider you choose.
