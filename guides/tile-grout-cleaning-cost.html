How Much Does Tile and Grout Cleaning Cost? 2026 Price Guide

Updated 2026-10-17 · Figures are typical estimates reported by the sources cited at the end of this page.

Tile itself is tough. Grout is the weak point. It is porous, it sits a little lower than the tile surface, and it collects whatever the mop pushes into it: soap scum and body oil in showers, cooking grease in kitchens, tracked-in dirt in entries. Once grout darkens, ordinary mopping just moves dirty water around, and the floor looks worn even when the tile is fine. Professional tile and grout cleaning uses alkaline cleaners, stiff brushes and hot-water or steam extraction to pull that soil back out, and usually ends with a sealer so it does not soak right back in. This guide compiles the 2026 ranges from Angi, HomeGuide and Thumbtack and explains what separates a cleaning job from a restoration or repair job.

Quick answer

Angi reports a 2026 national average of about $480 for professional tile and grout cleaning, with most homeowners paying $285–$676, based on what it describes as 7,656 verified projects. Angi lists rates of $0.50–$3 per square foot and minimum charges of $100–$200. HomeGuide cites $0.50–$3.50 per square foot, or about $250–$750 total on average, with hourly rates of $30–$50. Thumbtack reports a national grout cleaning range of $320–$764, with most people paying around $491, observed jobs from about $174 to $1,398, and per-square-foot pricing around $0.75–$3. These are typical estimates, not quotes.

Why small tiles cost more to clean than big ones

Pricing follows the grout, not the tile. Angi and HomeGuide both note that smaller tiles mean more grout lines per square foot, so a floor of 2-inch mosaic or penny tile takes far longer than the same area of 24-inch porcelain. Shower walls with small subway tile, niches, benches and corners are the slowest work of all because a floor machine cannot reach them and much of it is done by hand. If one company quotes your bathroom much higher than the kitchen next to it, tile size is often the reason. Angi also warns that large-format slabs carry their own risk: the wrong chemical can mark an entire tile, which is expensive to replace, so a careful cleaner may price conservatively until they know the material.

HomeGuide describes low-pressure steam combined with alkaline cleaning agents as the most effective approach and says steam does not damage grout when done properly, while acid-based cleaners and pressure washing can. Thumbtack describes typical professional service as a cleaning solution, a high-pressure rinse and a sealer. In practice many companies use a truck-mounted or portable extraction unit with a spinning hood that sprays hot water and vacuums it back up in one pass. Ask what the machine is, what chemical goes down first, and whether it is safe for your specific tile.

Natural stone is a different job

Marble, travertine, limestone and slate do not behave like glazed ceramic or porcelain. Many common grout cleaners, especially acidic ones, can etch calcium-based stone, leaving dull spots that need honing to remove. Thumbtack reminds homeowners to check labels because natural stone needs special care, and Angi lists natural stone repair at $5–$50 per square foot, compared with $1–$15 for ceramic and porcelain. If your floor or shower is stone, say so on the first call and ask whether the company uses stone-safe, pH-neutral products. A company that treats every floor the same is the wrong pick for stone.

Sealing. Angi recommends sealing cement-based grout after every cleaning and sealing tile about every three years, and notes epoxy grout does not need resealing. Angi and HomeGuide both say grout should be fully dry before it is sealed. HomeGuide estimates grout takes 24 to 72 hours to dry depending on humidity and grout type, and Angi advises waiting at least 24 hours, because sealer will not bond to damp grout. That means a same-visit "clean and seal" should use a product the company is confident in for that condition, or the seal should be applied on a return visit.

Recoloring. A color sealant is an opaque coating brushed into the joints. Angi notes it usually doubles as a sealer and is applied by hand, so it is labor-heavy. It is the usual fix for light grout that never looks clean or for patchy stains that cleaning will not lift.

Restoration vs. repair. Angi describes restoration as filling small cracks or replacing the top layer of grout, and repair as removing the old grout and starting over. HomeGuide prices full shower regrouting higher still, at $7–$28 per square foot. If grout is crumbling, missing or soft, cleaning it is wasted money. Get the repair price instead.

What else moves a quote

  • Time since the last cleaning. Angi and HomeGuide both report heavy-buildup surcharges of roughly $100–$200.
  • Furniture and appliances. Thumbtack notes some cleaners charge extra to move heavy items and may refuse antiques or a full china cabinet. Clearing the room yourself is the easiest saving.
  • Hand-only areas. Thumbtack prices hand or spot cleaning around $30–$50 per hour or more, the same band Angi and HomeGuide give for hourly work.
  • Access for equipment. Truck-mounted units need hose runs to the room. Upper floors and long runs can raise the price, according to Thumbtack.
  • Loose or cracked tiles. Re-bonding or replacing tiles is priced separately. Angi lists tile repair at $1–$50 per square foot depending on material.

DIY vs. hiring out

Angi puts hardware-store tools and sealant at about $25–$200 and professional cleaning at about $300–$500 per room. HomeGuide estimates DIY at $10–$150 depending on whether you buy a steamer. HomeGuide says the Tile Council of North America recommends alkaline cleaners and warns against undiluted bleach, ammonia-based cleaners, harsh acids, and oil- or wax-based products, which can damage grout or leave a film. DIY works on a small, lightly soiled bathroom floor with ceramic or porcelain tile. It is a poor trade on large floors, shower walls with years of soap scum, or any natural stone, where the risk of etching or a blotchy result outweighs the savings.

How often to clean

Angi recommends a professional deep cleaning one to two times a year, with routine cleaning in between, and HomeGuide suggests every 6 to 12 months depending on foot traffic, dirt and moisture. A sealed floor that is swept or vacuumed before mopping, and a shower that gets a squeegee after use, will need the pro less often, and Angi notes keeping up with routine cleaning is the simplest way to avoid the heavy-buildup surcharge.

How to compare tile and grout cleaning quotes

Tile and grout bids are easiest to compare when every company prices the same square footage and the same steps. Line them up on:

  • Measured area, room by room. Give every company the same measurements, including shower walls. Ask whether the minimum charge applies and whether bundling rooms lowers the rate.
  • Cleaning method and chemistry. Hand scrubbing, steam or hot-water extraction, and what goes down first. Make sure the cleaner is safe for your tile, and pH-neutral if any of it is natural stone.
  • Is sealing included? Thumbtack notes some quotes include a basic sealer and some don't. Ask which sealer, whether it's penetrating or color seal, and whether it goes on the same day or after the grout dries.
  • Heavy-buildup surcharge. Angi and HomeGuide both cite roughly $100–$200 extra for neglected grout. Ask for that number before the visit, not on the invoice.
  • Shower walls and caulk. Recaulking tub and shower joints is often a separate charge. Get it itemized if you want it done.
  • Furniture and appliances. Who moves the table, fridge or washer, and does it cost extra?
  • What they won't promise. Etched stone, dye stains and crumbling grout may not come clean. A good company tells you that before it starts.

Be wary of very low per-square-foot "specials" on a whole-house floor. Ask whether that price covers grout lines, or only a quick pass over the tile surface.

Notes for metro Atlanta homeowners

Two local conditions shape tile and grout cleaning around Atlanta. The first is humidity. Bathrooms without strong exhaust fans stay damp for hours after a shower in the summer, and that moisture keeps mildew growing in grout lines and caulk (see the EPA guide to mold and moisture for moisture-control basics). HomeGuide notes grout can take 24 to 72 hours to dry depending on humidity, so schedule sealing when you can run the AC and the bath fan, and don't let a crew seal grout that is still damp. If black spots come back weeks after cleaning, the fix is usually ventilation or failing caulk, not more scrubbing. The second is red clay. Clay tracked in from yards, job sites and dogs settles into the grout at entries, mudrooms and kitchen doors and leaves an orange-brown cast that ordinary mopping spreads around. Point those areas out when you ask for quotes, ask whether the company has dealt with clay staining before, and consider a darker grout color seal near doors that see daily traffic. Walk-off mats outside and inside entry doors, and a dry sweep before mopping, cut down how much clay reaches the grout. Outdoor tile on patios and pool decks also collects heavy spring pollen (check the Atlanta Allergy & Asthma pollen count). Rinse it off before it gets ground in, and ask whether the cleaner's sealer is rated for exterior use.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need to seal grout after cleaning?

For cement-based grout, usually yes. Angi recommends sealing grout after every cleaning, once it has fully dried, and notes epoxy grout doesn't need resealing.

How long before I can walk on the floor or use the shower?

It depends on the method and the sealer. HomeGuide says grout can take 24 to 72 hours to fully dry depending on humidity, and Angi advises waiting at least 24 hours before sealing. Ask your cleaner about foot traffic and shower use for the specific products they use.

Can bleach clean my grout?

HomeGuide, citing the Tile Council of North America, advises against undiluted bleach, ammonia-based cleaners and harsh acids on grout. It recommends alkaline cleaners instead. Natural stone needs stone-safe, pH-neutral products.
