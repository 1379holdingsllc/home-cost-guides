How Much Does Deck Staining Cost? 2026 Price Guide

Updated 2026-10-15 · Figures are typical estimates reported by the sources cited at the end of this page.

A deck is usually the largest piece of exposed wood on a house, and it takes more sun, rain and foot traffic than anything else outside. Staining is how you keep it from turning gray, splintering and soaking up water. Quotes vary because the work varies: a deck that was stained two years ago and only needs a wash and a fresh coat is a very different job from a neglected deck with peeling solid stain that has to be stripped first. This guide covers the published price ranges, what you're actually paying for, and how to tell which kind of job you have.

Quick answer

Angi reports a 2026 national average of about $850 to stain a deck, with most homeowners spending $550–$1,250. On a per-square-foot basis, Angi cites roughly $2–$4 per square foot for professional staining, with labor alone typically $1–$2.50 per square foot (up to about $7 in high-cost areas or for labor-intensive jobs). A well-maintained deck that only needs re-staining can come in much lower. Angi lists re-staining at about $0.50–$1 per square foot. All figures are typical estimates from that source, not quotes.

What you're actually paying for

The stain itself is a small part of the bill. Angi lists stain at roughly $20–$120 per gallon depending on type, and a gallon covers about 150–300 square feet depending on how thirsty the wood is. Most of the price is labor: moving furniture, cleaning, sanding, masking the house siding, cutting in around balusters, and applying stain evenly without lap marks. Balusters and stairs take far more time per square foot than open floorboards. A small deck with a lot of railing can cost more than a larger, plain platform.

1. Prep is the swing factor

Stain only performs as well as the surface underneath it. A typical prep sequence is cleaning (often a wood cleaner plus low-pressure rinse), letting the wood dry, then light sanding to knock down raised grain and splinters. If the old finish is a solid stain or paint that's peeling, it usually has to come off. Angi notes paint stripping can run $9–$12 per square foot, which can be more than the staining itself. If a bid seems far higher than the others, check whether it includes stripping that the cheaper bids skipped.

2. Stain type

  • Clear and transparent: show the most wood grain but offer the least UV protection, so they need recoating most often.
  • Semi-transparent: the common middle ground. It tints the wood, shows the grain, and soaks in rather than forming a film.
  • Semi-solid and solid: look more like paint and hide more of the grain and blemishes. They tend to last longer on vertical surfaces, but on floorboards they can wear and peel in walkways, and once they peel they're harder to redo.
  • Oil vs. water-based: oil-based products generally soak in deeper. Water-based products dry faster, clean up with water and are lower-odor. Your contractor's preference often comes from what has held up on decks in your climate. Ask why they chose it.

Switching from a solid stain to a transparent one is usually not possible without stripping. Pick the look you want to keep long term.

3. Wood type and age

Pressure-treated pine is the most common deck lumber. New pressure-treated wood needs time to dry out before it will absorb stain. Angi suggests a simple test: sprinkle water on the boards, and if it beads up, the wood isn't ready. Dense hardwoods such as ipe or mahogany need penetrating oils made for them, and porous softwoods can absorb unevenly. Composite decking isn't stained at all. It's cleaned. If your deck is composite, you don't need this service.

4. Repairs found along the way

Cleaning often exposes problems: soft boards, popped nails, loose railings or rot at the ledger. Staining over rot doesn't fix it. A good contractor will point out loose or soft areas before staining and price board replacement separately. If the structure is questionable, especially where the deck attaches to the house, have it inspected before you spend money on a finish.

Sealing vs. staining

Terminology is muddled in this trade. Many modern deck stains are already water-repellent, and some contractors use "seal" to mean "stain." Angi treats sealing as a separate clear protective step that it prices at about $550–$1,300 on its own. When comparing bids, ask exactly what product goes on, how many coats, and whether a separate clear sealer is involved. Otherwise one bid may look cheaper simply because it's one fewer step.

How often you'll pay this

Angi suggests re-staining roughly every two to three years and notes a good stain typically lasts two to five years depending on product, sun and foot traffic. Two signs it's time: water soaks into the boards instead of beading, and the color has faded unevenly, especially on stair treads and in front of doors. Staying on schedule is the main way to keep costs down. A deck re-stained before the finish fails usually needs only cleaning and a coat, not sanding and stripping.

DIY or hire out?

Deck staining is a common DIY job, and the materials are a fraction of the cost. What makes it hard is weather and prep. You need several dry days in a row: one to clean, time for the wood to dry, and a dry window after application. Rented pressure washers can easily gouge soft pine. If you DIY, use the lowest pressure that works, keep the tip moving with the grain, and test the stain on a hidden board first. Hiring out makes the most sense for large, multi-level or railing-heavy decks, and for anything that needs stripping.

How to compare deck staining quotes

Deck staining bids usually differ in prep, not paint. To compare them:

  • Prep steps, spelled out. Cleaner and rinse, brightener, sanding, stripping. Which ones are included? A bid without stripping isn't comparable to one with it.
  • Product, color and coats. Get the brand, product line and opacity in writing, and how many coats. Make sure a separate sealer isn't silently missing from one bid.
  • What surfaces are counted. Floorboards only, or rails, balusters, stairs, skirting and the underside of rails too?
  • Weather plan. How many dry days do they need before and after, and what happens if rain comes early?
  • Repairs. Ask for a per-board replacement price up front, so a "few loose boards" doesn't turn into an open-ended add-on.
  • Protection. How they will mask siding, brick, door thresholds and plants from overspray and drips.
  • Warranty. Many finishes carry a limited warranty. Ask what the contractor stands behind and for how long.

Notes for metro Atlanta homeowners

Atlanta's climate is hard on deck finishes: strong summer sun, high humidity that encourages mildew on shaded boards, heavy spring pollen that sticks to wet stain, and frequent afternoon thunderstorms in summer. Spring and fall are usually the easiest seasons to find dry stretches with moderate temperatures. Check the National Weather Service Peachtree City office forecast and the Atlanta Allergy & Asthma pollen count before locking in a date. Most metro-area decks are pressure-treated pine, often on sloped lots with tall stairs and lots of railing, which adds labor. Leaves and pine straw left sitting on boards hold moisture and stain the finish, so regular sweeping goes a long way toward stretching the time between re-stains.

Frequently asked questions

Can I stain a new pressure-treated deck right away?

Usually not. The wood needs time to dry. If water beads on the surface, wait and test again later.

Should I stain or paint my deck?

Stain soaks in and shows wood texture. Paint and solid stain form a film that can peel on walking surfaces. Many owners use penetrating stain on floors and solid color on rails.
