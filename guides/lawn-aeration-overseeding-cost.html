How Much Does Lawn Aeration and Overseeding Cost? 2026 Price Guide

Updated 2026-10-20 · Figures are typical estimates reported by the sources cited at the end of this page.

Every fall, door hangers and postcards start offering to "aerate and seed" your lawn. For a thin fescue yard, that combination is one of the most useful things you can pay for: a machine pulls thousands of small soil plugs, and new seed drops into the holes where it actually touches dirt. But prices are all over the place, the two services are often quoted separately, and the right timing depends on which grass you have. This guide pulls together what published cost guides report for 2026, explains what each part of the job costs, and covers the timing that University of Georgia turf specialists recommend.

Quick answer

Angi reports a 2026 national average of about $141 for lawn aeration, based on 3,222 projects, with most homeowners paying $76–$207. Angi says adding overseeding, fertilization or sprinkler marking adds roughly $150–$415. HomeGuide puts aeration at $100–$300 and the combined aerate-and-overseed job at $160–$425 for 10,000 square feet, depending on grass type. HomeGuide's overseeding cost guide gives a wider range for the combination, $200–$800 or $0.04–$0.08 per square foot for 5,000 to 10,000 square feet. Thumbtack reports aeration at $159–$452 nationally, with most people paying around $268. These are typical estimates from those sources, not quotes.

Core aeration is what most lawn companies use. HomeGuide calls it the most effective way to relieve compaction, and Angi says it's the best choice for clay-heavy or heavily compacted soil. A spike aerator only pokes holes, and HomeGuide notes it may cause more compaction. Liquid aeration needs multiple applications and, per HomeGuide, takes 45 to 60 days to show results. If you're overseeding, ask for core aeration. The open holes are what give the seed its soil contact.

What overseeding adds

Overseeding means spreading new seed over an existing lawn to thicken it, without killing the old grass first. HomeGuide puts overseeding or reseeding at $0.04–$0.18 per square foot, or $40–$180 per 1,000 square feet, with minimum service fees of $125–$400. Angi's reseeding guide reports a higher 2026 national average of $1,083, with most homeowners paying $438–$1,758 and per-square-foot pricing of $0.10–$0.20. Angi's figures cover seeding and reseeding projects in general, while HomeGuide's overseeding range covers spreading seed on an existing lawn, so check which kind of job a quote describes.

Seed itself is a small part of the bill. HomeGuide lists fescue seed at $1.50–$10 per pound, and Angi puts fescue at about $3 per pound, one of the cheapest options, with a 50-pound bag at $100–$250. Thumbtack reports seeding as an add-on at about $150–$600, with most homeowners paying around $300.

Why bundling is usually cheaper

Booking both services on the same visit is where most homeowners save. The crew and machine are already on the lawn, and the seed goes down while the holes are fresh. HomeGuide says seeding right after aerating is ideal because the open soil gives seed a good place to root. HomeGuide also lists national lawn chains' combined core aeration and overseeding at $200–$300 per treatment for TruGreen and $350–$900 for Lawn Doctor. Thumbtack gives one published example of a local pro charging $150 to aerate, seed and fertilize up to 5,000 square feet. Prices like that are a useful sanity check when a quote for the same lawn comes in at several times the amount.

Other add-ons and what they cost

  • Fertilizer: Angi lists fertilization after aeration at $70–$380. HomeGuide puts a fertilizer treatment at $65–$200 and Thumbtack at $35–$180, with most people paying around $80 for a 5,000-square-foot yard.
  • Sprinkler marking: Aerator tines can damage shallow irrigation heads and lines. Angi lists irrigation marking or repair at $135–$415, and HomeGuide notes some pros charge extra to flag sprinkler heads, shallow lines and buried cables.
  • Mowing and debris cleanup first: HomeGuide says most companies charge extra if they have to mow or clear debris before aerating. Thumbtack says aeration works best on moist soil and freshly mowed grass.
  • Dethatching: HomeGuide puts dethatching at $100–$300 on its aeration page and $75–$300 on its seeding page.
  • Topdressing: Angi lists compost or soil topdressing at $30–$150 per cubic yard.
  • Soil test: HomeGuide puts a soil test at $10–$40. It tells you whether lime or fertilizer is needed before you pay for seed.

Timing matters as much as price

The best time to aerate and seed depends on your grass. For tall fescue, which UGA says is best adapted to Georgia's Piedmont region, where metro Atlanta sits, a UGA Extension fall gardening publication says September and October are the ideal months to seed, with September the best. Seeded earlier, young grass faces heat stress and disease. Seeded later, it's exposed to cold. UGA says that under good conditions fescue seed germinates in 5 to 10 days and is ready for its first mowing in 2 to 3 weeks. If fescue can't be seeded before mid-November, UGA suggests waiting until mid-February, and it advises against seeding in December or after April because the plants won't develop deep enough roots to survive Georgia's summer.

Warm-season lawns are different. UGA's lawn calendars mark May through August as the best months to core aerate Bermudagrass and zoysiagrass, when those grasses are actively growing. A fall aerate-and-seed package is mainly a fescue service. Bermuda lawns can be overseeded with ryegrass for winter color, which UGA's calendar marks as best in October, but UGA's zoysia calendar says overseeding zoysia with ryegrass is not recommended.

Aftercare is part of the cost

New seed has to stay moist to sprout. UGA recommends keeping the top 1 to 2 inches of soil moist but not wet, which usually means daily watering of up to a quarter inch for the first three weeks, then watering more deeply and less often as seedlings develop. Budget for that water, or for a sprinkler timer if you don't have one. HomeGuide says to wait 1 to 3 weeks after aeration, or until the grass is at least 3 inches tall, before mowing, and Thumbtack says the soil plugs left on the lawn usually break down in about two weeks. Don't rake them up. Falling leaves are the other fall issue: a thick mat of leaves on new seedlings blocks light, so plan light, frequent leaf cleanup until the new grass is established.

DIY vs. hiring a pro

Angi says renting an aerator for a day costs about $80–$120, compared with pro labor of $40–$75 per hour, and that DIY makes the most sense for small, flat yards when you're comfortable running heavy equipment. Core aerators are heavy and hard to steer on slopes. Angi's reseeding guide estimates DIY seeding saves about $250–$350 in labor. The case for hiring is commercial-grade equipment, an even seed rate and someone who knows to flag sprinkler heads before the tines go in. If you DIY, UGA recommends certified seed (identified by a blue tag) and splitting the seed into two passes at right angles to avoid gaps.

How often do you need it?

HomeGuide says aerating once a year is enough for most residential lawns, with heavy clay or high-traffic lawns sometimes needing it twice a year. HomeGuide also notes that combining core aeration with overseeding is typically only necessary every 3 to 4 years. UGA notes that established tall fescue lawns tend to thin and become clumpy and may need periodic reseeding every few years, so a fescue lawn may need seed more often than that general rule suggests. If your lawn is thick and water soaks in instead of running off, you may be able to skip a year.

How to compare lawn aeration and overseeding quotes

Fall aeration and seeding offers often look alike on a door hanger and turn out to be very different jobs. Before you compare prices, get every company to answer the same questions:

  • Core or spike? Ask for core (plug) aeration if you're seeding. Spike and liquid aeration are cheaper but don't open the soil the same way.
  • How many passes? One pass over the lawn or two in different directions? More holes means more places for seed to land, and more machine time.
  • What seed, and how much? Get the grass type, whether it's certified (blue-tag) seed, and the rate in pounds per 1,000 square feet. UGA's tall fescue calendar lists 3 to 5 pounds per 1,000 square feet for interseeding an established lawn. A cheap quote that skimps on seed may only look cheaper.
  • Is fertilizer included? Starter fertilizer, lime or nothing? If they offer lime or fertilizer, ask whether it's based on a soil test.
  • Weed control timing. UGA warns against applying a preemergence herbicide if the lawn will be seeded within 3 to 4 months, because it can stop the new seed too. Make sure a fall weed-control program and the seeding don't conflict.
  • Sprinklers and invisible fences. Will they flag irrigation heads and buried lines before aerating, and who pays if a head gets broken?
  • How was the lawn measured? Quotes by the square foot only line up if everyone used the same number. Ask for the square footage on every bid.
  • Watering instructions. A good company tells you how and how long to water. Ask if they offer any germination guarantee and what it requires from you.

If one company quotes aeration alone and another quotes the full package, price the extras separately so you're comparing the same work.

Notes for metro Atlanta homeowners

Metro Atlanta sits in the zone where both cool- and warm-season grasses grow, so the first question for any fall aeration offer is which grass you have. According to UGA's Lawns in Georgia: Selection and Species publication, tall fescue is best adapted to Georgia's Piedmont, and its southern limit is roughly Griffin, although it can grow farther south in shade. The same publication notes that established fescue lawns tend to thin and get clumpy and may need reseeding every few years. That's why the fall aerate-and-seed visit is so common in fescue neighborhoods. The UGA Extension tall fescue planting guidance says September and October are ideal for seeding, with September best, and the UGA tall fescue lawn calendar marks October, March and April as the best months to core aerate fescue. If your lawn is Bermuda or zoysia, UGA's calendars put core aeration in May through August instead, so a fall aeration on those lawns isn't the timing UGA recommends. Georgia's clay soils are another reason aeration gets pitched here: Angi says core aeration is the best choice for clay-heavy soil, and HomeGuide says heavy clay lawns may need aerating twice a year. Watering a new lawn is allowed under Georgia's rules. UGA notes the 2010 Water Stewardship Act allows proper irrigation to establish seed, and its fescue calendar says watering is allowed for 30 days on newly planted seed or sod. In a hilly yard, expect a higher quote, since HomeGuide says slopes are harder to work with aeration equipment.

Frequently asked questions

Should I rake up the soil plugs after aeration?

No. Thumbtack says the plugs usually break down in about two weeks, depending on the weather, and that they help break down thatch and feed the soil as they decompose.

How soon can I mow after aerating and seeding?

HomeGuide says to wait 1 to 3 weeks after aeration, or until the grass is at least 3 inches tall. For new fescue seedlings, UGA Extension says to start mowing at a height of 2 inches, avoid mowing when the grass is wet, and raise the height to 2.5 to 3 inches as the seedlings mature.

Is it too late to seed fescue in November?

Seeding gets riskier as the weather cools. UGA Extension says September and October are ideal, and if tall fescue is going to be seeded after mid-November, it suggests waiting until mid-February instead. It doesn't recommend seeding in December.
