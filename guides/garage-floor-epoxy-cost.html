How Much Does Garage Floor Epoxy Cost? 2026 Price Guide

Updated 2026-10-20 · Figures are typical estimates reported by the sources cited at the end of this page.

An epoxy garage floor is a hard, glossy resin coating bonded to the concrete slab. Done well, it resists oil drips, cleans with a squeegee, and hides the dusty gray concrete that tracks into the house. Done badly, it peels in sheets where the car tires sit. The price difference between those two outcomes is mostly prep work and the type of resin used, and that's why garage floor quotes can be double or half what a neighbor paid for the same size garage. This guide lays out the published cost ranges, what's included in a professional install, and the questions that separate a lasting floor from one that fails in a year or two.

Quick answer

Angi reports epoxy flooring at $2–$12 per square foot installed, including materials and labor, with an average project of about $2,526 and a typical range of $750–$5,800. HomeGuide reports $4–$10 per square foot installed and $1,600–$5,800 for a two-car garage. Thumbtack reports a national range of $1,703–$3,280 for epoxy garage floor coatings, with most homeowners paying around $2,370, and lists low- and high-end projects at $1,046 and $5,261. All of these are typical estimates from those sources, not quotes.

Materials vs. labor

HomeGuide splits the installed price into $1–$5 per square foot for materials and $3–$5 for labor. Angi says labor is about 33% to 60% of a professional install, or roughly $1–$7 per square foot or $50–$150 per hour, and that most companies send a two-person crew. Labor rises with the amount of grinding, crack filling and coating layers the floor needs. HomeGuide notes that solid epoxy also takes more labor to apply than water-based.

Concrete prep and repairs

Epoxy is only as good as its bond to the slab. Expect some or all of these on a quote:

  • Surface profiling. HomeGuide says most products need acid washing or etching first to give the floor "teeth." Angi puts acid washing, power washing or etching at about $50–$100 when it's billed separately. The Thumbtack pro uses diamond grinding plus HEPA dust collection instead, and lists shot-blasting, glue or mastic removal and old-coating removal as other possible prep steps.
  • Cleaning. HomeGuide lists power washing at $0.35–$0.77 per square foot. Oil-soaked spots have to be degreased or ground out, or the coating lifts there first.
  • Crack and spall repair. Angi lists basic concrete patching at $25–$250. If the damage is widespread, Angi lists resurfacing at $1,000–$5,000 and HomeGuide at $3–$7 per square foot. Angi cautions that some cracks point to a structural problem, with foundation repair at $2,150–$7,730. Settle that question before you coat the floor.
  • Emptying the garage. The floor has to be completely clear. Angi budgets at least $100 if you need a storage unit, and HomeGuide notes that clearing it yourself can lower the installer's fee.

Flakes, color and topcoats

Most garage systems are more than one clear coat. The Thumbtack article describes decorative vinyl chips (also called color flakes), from 1/32 inch to 1 inch, broadcast into the wet resin. A full broadcast adds strength and hides imperfections, and textured chips add grip. Angi lists flakes at roughly $0.04 per square foot and notes that a sealant layer can be added over the cured epoxy. HomeGuide says pebbles, metallic tints and custom designs all cost extra. Angi's main cost-saving tip is to choose a basic coating over a decorative one.

Cure time and the two-day rule

The Thumbtack pro calls epoxy at minimum a two-day job: a primer coat goes down on day one and has to dry overnight before the next coat. He warns against anyone who primes in the morning and topcoats after lunch, and says manufacturers won't warranty the product without a primer coat. After the final coat, Thumbtack says you can walk on it in 24–36 hours, set down items like a lawnmower after 48 hours, and park a car after 5–7 days. HomeGuide gives similar windows: no foot traffic for 16–24 hours and no driving for 2–7 days. Plan on parking in the driveway for about a week.

DIY kits vs. hiring a pro

HomeGuide prices DIY at $50–$600 for raw materials or $200–$700 for premade kits. Angi puts DIY at $2–$5 per square foot once you buy the specialized tools. Thumbtack argues that hiring a pro often costs only a small fraction more than doing it yourself, since pros own the grinders and know how much product a floor needs. HomeGuide says water-based epoxy is manageable for a homeowner, but solid epoxy should be left to professionals. A realistic DIY plan is a water-based kit on a clean, sound slab, accepting the shorter life.

Alternatives worth pricing

If epoxy doesn't fit your budget or timeline, HomeGuide lists acrylic concrete sealer at $1–$2 per square foot (reapplied every 2–3 years) and polyurethane at $1.25–$3, which it says is stronger, cures faster and can go down in more temperatures. Angi lists concrete painting at $1.50–$3 per square foot, and HomeGuide lists polished concrete at $3–$15+. Angi also mentions polyurea coatings as a scratch-resistant option. Ask each contractor which systems they install so you're comparing like with like.

Keeping it looking new

Angi recommends regular sweeping or vacuuming, then mopping with warm water and a mild cleaner that's safe for the epoxy, and avoiding soap-based or harsh chemical cleaners that break the surface down. Thumbtack suggests squeegeeing water off to keep the floor glossy for 8–10 years, and HomeGuide suggests mats at the entrances and keeping the garage door closed when you can.

How to compare garage floor epoxy quotes

Garage floor coating quotes vary as much in the product and prep as in the price. Ask every installer the same questions, in writing:

  • Which resin, exactly? Water-based, solvent-based or 100% solids epoxy, or a polyurea or polyaspartic system. Ask for the product data sheet.
  • How will the slab be prepared? Acid etch, diamond grinding or shot-blasting. Grinding with dust collection is a different level of prep than an acid wash.
  • Moisture testing. HomeGuide lists "Will you test for moisture in the flooring first?" as a question to ask. Moisture coming up through the slab is a common reason coatings fail.
  • Crack, pit and joint repair. Is it included, priced per foot, or billed at an hourly rate? Get the method and the price before work starts.
  • Number of coats and days. Primer, base, broadcast and topcoat, and whether the primer gets an overnight cure. A one-day job is a different system than a two-day job, so compare it accordingly.
  • Flake coverage. Light scatter or full broadcast, which changes appearance, texture and material cost.
  • Square footage measured. Make sure every bid covers the same area, including stoops, steps and any storage nook.
  • Warranty terms. What it covers (peeling, hot-tire lift, staining), who backs it, and for how long. Thumbtack warns that very long warranties mean little if the company doesn't last.

Be cautious of very low "garage special" prices. Ask whether the price assumes a perfect slab with no grinding or repairs, and what the price becomes if the floor needs more.

Notes for metro Atlanta homeowners

Humidity is the main local factor for epoxy around Atlanta. HomeGuide says coatings should go down only between 60° and 85°F and below 85% humidity, and that epoxy cannot bond properly when the surface is wet or the air is very humid. Garages aren't air-conditioned, so on muggy summer days a slab can collect condensation, especially early in the morning. Ask your installer how they check slab and air conditions on install day, and expect them to reschedule rather than coat a damp floor. Spring and fall usually offer more installable days than the middle of summer. Watch the National Weather Service Peachtree City office forecast, too, because rain blown under the door during cure can mark the surface. Georgia red clay is the other factor. Clay tracked in on tires and boots grinds into bare concrete and stains it orange, and pros will need to clean or grind that out during prep. Ask how they handle clay staining and whether a flake blend in earth tones would hide it between cleanings. After the install, HomeGuide's tip about mats at the entrances helps keep clay from being ground into the finish. Many metro Atlanta garages have a stoop step into the house and a slight slope toward the door for drainage. Confirm whether the step and the threshold edge are in the quoted square footage. If the slab has a crack that keeps widening, have it looked at before you coat it. Covering a cracking slab with epoxy won't fix it.

Frequently asked questions

How long until I can park on a new epoxy floor?

Thumbtack says to wait 5 to 7 days before bringing a vehicle in, and HomeGuide says 2 to 7 days depending on the product. Light foot traffic is usually fine after about a day.

How long does an epoxy garage floor last?

HomeGuide says water-based epoxy lasts about 3 to 5 years, while solid epoxy can last 10 to 20 years. Angi gives 10 to 20 years for a properly prepared, professionally installed floor.

Can epoxy go over an old coating?

Usually the old coating has to come off first. Thumbtack's example lists removing a failed water-based coating as extra prep, and the pro grinds the concrete before priming so the new system bonds to the slab.
