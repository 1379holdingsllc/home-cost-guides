How Much Does an Estate Cleanout Cost? 2026 Price Guide

Updated 2026-10-17 · Figures are typical estimates reported by the sources cited at the end of this page.

An estate cleanout usually lands on a family at a hard time. Someone has passed away or moved into assisted living, the house has to be emptied before it can be sold or rented, and decades of furniture, paperwork, clothes and garage contents have to go somewhere. Pricing is confusing because "estate cleanout" can mean anything from two guys and a truck hauling what's left after the family has taken their keepsakes, to a full-service company that sorts every drawer, sells what has value, donates the rest and leaves the house broom clean. This guide breaks down what the published sources report for each piece of that work so you can tell which kind of quote you're looking at.

Quick answer

Angi reports a 2026 national average of about $1,250 for estate cleanout services, with most people spending $275–$4,000 and complex projects reaching about $6,000. Angi says cleanouts usually aren't priced per square foot. The number of items, especially bulky ones, matters more than the size of the house. For a heavily cluttered home, HomeGuide's hoarding cleanup figures are a useful benchmark: $1–$2 per square foot, or $1,000–$4,000 total, with hourly pricing of $25–$100. These are typical estimates from those sources, not quotes.

What each part of the job costs

Angi breaks estate work into separate services. Seeing them side by side explains why two quotes for the same house can be thousands of dollars apart: one company may be pricing only the hauling, while another is pricing appraisal and sales work too.

Labor is the base of every quote. Angi lists professional movers at $25–$50 per hour and house cleaning services at $25–$200 per hour for estate work, with total cost rising with the number of rooms and the volume of belongings.

How sale commissions change the math

When a company sells items for the estate, it usually doesn't bill a flat fee for that part. Angi, citing the American Society of Estate Liquidators, says sale commissions often run 35%–40% of gross proceeds. That means the cost depends on what sells. If the contents bring in $3,000, a 40% commission is $1,200, and the estate keeps the rest. If very little sells, the commission is small, but the company still has to clear what's left, which is usually billed as hauling. Before signing, ask three things: what percentage they keep, whether unsold items are removed as part of the same agreement or priced separately, and when and how the estate gets paid.

Bulky and special items

Heavy, awkward or regulated items add cost because they take more people, more time or special disposal. Angi's 2026 figures:

Walk the house before you call and list the heavy pieces: pianos, safes, gun cabinets, chest freezers, workbenches, and anything stored in a basement or upstairs. Paint cans, pool chemicals, old fuel and motor oil in the garage often can't go in a regular truckload, so mention them up front.

The costs people forget to budget

Emptying the house is rarely the last expense. Angi lists these common add-ons, which some cleanout companies include, some subcontract, and some don't offer at all:

  • Junk removal: $130–$370
  • Dumpster rental: $290–$480
  • House cleaning after the cleanout: $70–$370

If the house is going on the market, the final cleaning matters. Once furniture is gone, you'll see dust lines, carpet wear and wall marks that were hidden before. A deep or move-out clean after the haul-away is a separate job from the cleanout itself.

When it's really a hoarding cleanup

Some estates are much harder than a normal cleanout. HomeGuide describes the five-level Clutter-Hoarding Scale from the Institute for Challenging Disorganization, from Level 1 (minor clutter, doorways clear) to Level 5 (unsanitary, possibly unlivable, with pests, mold or failed plumbing). The higher the level, the more the job looks like specialized cleanup. HomeGuide lists these totals by home size:

HomeGuide says cleanup companies typically include disposal in that price, while junk disposal on its own costs $150–$350 per load. If there's biohazard material, such as animal waste, sewage or an unattended death, HomeGuide lists biohazard cleanup at $1,500–$5,000, and that work needs a company trained for it. HomeGuide also says a hoarding cleanup usually takes one to five days.

Before anyone starts hauling

A few steps protect the family and the estate, and they also make the job faster and cheaper:

  • Confirm who has authority. Make sure the person hiring the company is the one entitled to dispose of the property, such as the executor or administrator, and that other heirs know the plan.
  • Pull documents first. Angi suggests gathering wills, medical records and financial statements before the crew arrives. Search drawers, closets, books and coat pockets. Cash, titles, deeds and bank records turn up in strange places.
  • Use the four-box method. Angi describes sorting into four groups: dispose, give away, keep and relocate. Every item the family sorts ahead of time is labor you don't pay for.
  • Photograph the rooms. Angi recommends photos of rooms and large items so companies can estimate scope accurately, which helps you get comparable remote quotes.
  • Set aside valuables and fragile items. Keep jewelry, collectibles and heirlooms out of the work area until they've been appraised or claimed.
  • Clear paths. Angi notes that clearing pathways lets movers work faster through the home.

Ways to keep the cost down

Angi suggests asking about discounts, including for veterans and first responders, and asking whether a company will buy valuable items such as jewelry, memorabilia or collectibles, which can offset part of the bill. The biggest savings usually come from doing the sorting yourselves, using free municipal bulk pickup for furniture where it's available, and booking cleanout, hauling and final cleaning together so you aren't paying several trip minimums.

How to compare estate cleanout quotes

Estate cleanout bids are only comparable when every company is quoting the same scope. Before you compare totals, get each one to answer in writing:

  • Haul-only or full service? Is the price just removal, or does it include sorting, donating, selling and a final clean? Angi prices these as separate services, so a low bid may simply cover less.
  • Sale terms. If they sell items, what commission do they keep, how are unsold items handled, and when does the estate get paid?
  • Disposal and donation. Are dump fees, donation drop-offs and recycling included? Ask for donation receipts if the estate wants them.
  • Exclusions. Which items won't they take (paint, chemicals, tires, propane tanks) and what does special handling cost?
  • Bulky items. Are the piano, safe, freezer and basement contents in the price, or extra per item or per staircase?
  • Timeline. How many days, how many workers, and what happens if the job runs long on an hourly bid?
  • Condition at the end. Broom swept, or cleaned and ready to list? Get that in writing.
  • Who signs. Make sure the contract is with the person who has authority over the estate.

A walkthrough quote is worth more than a phone estimate here. Angi says the volume of belongings, not the square footage, drives the price, and that's hard to judge without seeing the rooms or at least detailed photos.

Notes for metro Atlanta homeowners

If the home is inside the City of Atlanta, free city bulk pickup can take some of the furniture load off a paid hauler. According to the ATL311 Bulk Collection Request page, the Department of Public Works gives single-family residents 12 free scheduled bulk collections per year, on the regular weekly collection day. Each collection is limited to 8 cubic yards, about a small room of furniture, and anything beyond that counts as an additional collection. Items must be at the curb in front of the property, not before 7 p.m. the night before. Pickups have to be scheduled at least a business day ahead and canceled by 3 p.m. the day before, and a fee applies once the 12 are used up. One detail matters for estates: ATL311 says bulk collections can only be scheduled by the resident of the reported location, so an out-of-town executor may need help from someone who lives there, or may simply need a private hauler. The City of Atlanta Office of Solid Waste Services lists what's eligible. Auto parts and tires aren't collected, and yard trimmings have to be kept in a separate pile. Cities and counties outside Atlanta, including Cobb, DeKalb, Gwinnett, Clayton and Henry, set their own rules, and many unincorporated areas rely on private trash haulers, so check before you plan around curb pickup. Basements, crawlspaces and detached garages are common in older metro homes, and stairs and tight access add time. Mention them when you request quotes.

Frequently asked questions

How long does an estate cleanout take?

It depends on volume and how much sorting the family has already done. HomeGuide says even a heavily cluttered home usually takes one to five days with a full crew. A house the family has already sorted can often be cleared much faster.

Will an estate cleanout company pay me for valuable items?

Some will. Angi suggests asking whether a company buys items such as jewelry, memorabilia or collectibles, which can offset the bill. Companies that sell items for the estate usually keep a commission, which Angi reports often runs 35–40 percent of gross proceeds.

Should the house be cleaned before it's listed?

Usually, yes. Once furniture is out, dust, wall marks and floor wear show. Angi lists house cleaning after a cleanout at about $70–$370, and it's typically booked separately.

Are these prices guaranteed?

No. All figures on this page are typical ranges reported by the sources listed below, presented as estimates. Your actual price depends on your property, your location and the provider you choose.
