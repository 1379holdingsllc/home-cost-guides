How Much Does Pine Straw Installation Cost? 2026 Price Guide

Updated 2026-10-19 · Figures are typical estimates reported by the sources cited at the end of this page.

Drive through almost any neighborhood around Atlanta and you'll see it: reddish-brown pine straw tucked around azaleas, foundation shrubs and tree rings. It's the default bed covering across much of the Southeast because it's cheap, light and holds on slopes. Hiring a crew to spread it is a routine yard job, but quotes are confusing because pine straw is sold by the bale and bales aren't a standard size. This guide pulls together what published sources report for 2026, shows how to turn a per-bale quote into something you can compare, and explains what separates a good install from a sloppy one.

Quick answer

GreenPal reports that installed pine straw costs about $6–$14 per bale in 2026, with most residential jobs landing at $8–$10 per bale. GreenPal says a typical Southeastern property with 1,000 to 1,500 square feet of beds takes 25 to 40 bales, so a full application runs about $200–$400, including material, delivery, spreading, edge tucking and blowing off the driveway. On a per-square-foot basis, GreenPal puts most professional installs at $0.16–$0.35 per square foot at a settled depth of 2 to 3 inches, with most jobs at $0.20–$0.25. For the straw alone, Angi lists pine straw at $2.50–$10 per bale. These are typical estimates from those sources, not quotes.

Why the per-bale price can mislead you

Bark mulch is sold by the cubic yard, a fixed volume. Pine straw isn't. GreenPal notes that no standard bale size exists. Citing NC State Extension, it says bales generally run 24 to 30 inches long and weigh anywhere from 8 to 25 pounds dry, depending on the producer. Two crews can both quote "a bale" and mean very different amounts of straw. GreenPal's example: an $8 bale that covers 35 square feet costs about $0.23 per square foot, while a $13 bale covering 70 square feet costs about $0.19, so the cheaper bale makes the more expensive job.

The fix is simple. Ask each installer for the price per bale and the number of bales the job needs, multiply them, and divide by your bed square footage. GreenPal shows two quotes on a 1,200-square-foot bed, 30 bales at $9 ($270) and 24 bales at $11 ($264), where the one with the higher bale price costs less overall. Some crews sell machine-rolled straw instead. GreenPal says one roll holds about as much as 2 to 2.5 square bales and covers 80 to 125 square feet.

What you're paying for

GreenPal says raw pine straw sells at garden centers and landscape supply yards for about $3–$5 a bale, and that material is typically only 35%–45% of an installed quote. The rest is labor and overhead: picking up and hauling the bales, spreading to an even depth, tucking the edges, blowing straw and dust off hard surfaces, and raking out matted old straw or weeds when needed. GreenPal says an experienced installer working open beds near the truck can spread and finish 15 to 25 bales an hour, but output drops to 8 to 12 bales per worker per hour when every bale has to be carried into a fenced backyard, worked around dense plantings or pinned on a slope. That's why a backyard-heavy job costs more per bale than front beds off the driveway.

Longleaf vs. slash straw

Most baled straw comes from two trees. According to a University of Georgia article, slash and loblolly pines produce almost three-quarters of the straw sold in Georgia, while longleaf pine produces the longest and most desirable needles, which cost more and last longer in the landscape. GreenPal puts the premium at about $1–$2 more per bale for longleaf before installation. The trade-off is how often you redo it. GreenPal says longleaf holds its color for 6 to 12 months, so once a year is the usual cycle, while slash fades to gray in 4 to 6 months and is often refreshed twice a year. If you'd be paying two sets of delivery and labor charges for slash, longleaf can come out cheaper per year. GreenPal also says longer needles interlock better, so longleaf holds a bank better.

Whatever type you buy, UGA's advice is to look for fresh, clean straw: bales should be bright reddish-brown and flexible, without weeds, pine cones or other grasses mixed in. Pine cones add bulk to a bale but do nothing as mulch.

How deep, and do you remove the old straw?

A UGA Extension article on using pine straw recommends replenishing rather than replacing: leave the old straw in place, since it adds organic matter as it breaks down, and add new straw on top for a layer at least 2 to 4 inches thick. It says more than about 6 inches won't do any more good, and that 4 to 5 inches of fresh straw settles to about 2 inches. That's good news for your budget, because a refresh over existing straw takes fewer bales than a first install on bare soil. GreenPal says a refresh is typically 1 to 2 inches instead of a full 2 to 3, and recommends asking which one your quote is priced as. The article also advises keeping straw pulled back from shrub stems, especially azaleas, and spreading it out past the drip line where the feeder roots are.

Pine straw vs. bark mulch on cost

HomeGuide puts bark and wood mulch at $25–$100 per cubic yard for material, or $50–$150 per cubic yard installed, with one cubic yard covering about 108 square feet at 3 inches deep and delivery fees of $50–$150 when they're billed separately. Angi says the average cost to mulch a yard is about $180. Wood mulch lasts longer, and Angi rates it better for weed suppression and moisture retention. Pine straw is lighter and faster to spread, holds slopes better and, per Angi, needs replacing at least once a year. GreenPal says pine straw still comes out cheaper per square foot on an installed basis. If you're weighing the two, compare the cost per year, not just the cost per application.

DIY vs. hiring a crew

Spreading straw takes no special equipment, so it's one of the easier landscape jobs to do yourself. GreenPal estimates the DIY savings at about $90–$210. The work is in the hauling: GreenPal notes that 30 bales is more than one load in a standard pickup bed, and at a beginner pace of 8 to 12 bales an hour a 30-bale job takes most of a Saturday once you count the supply-yard trips. If you DIY, buy a few extra bales. GreenPal recommends adding 10%–15% to your calculated count for slopes, curved edges and settling.

What a good install includes

GreenPal describes a complete job as weeds and debris cleared first, an optional granular pre-emergent herbicide under the straw, straw spread evenly to a settled 2 to 3 inches (3 to 4 on bare soil), straw kept 2 to 3 inches back from trunks and stems, edges tucked with a spade or rake so the bed holds a clean line, and driveways, walks and patios blown clean. Skipping the tuck shows up within a week, when the wind pushes straw onto the driveway. Piling straw against trunks takes longer to show, but it traps moisture against the bark.

How to compare pine straw installation quotes

Pine straw quotes are only comparable when you convert them to the same unit. Before choosing a crew, get each one to tell you:

  • Bales and coverage. How many bales will the job take, and how many square feet of bed does that cover? Divide the total price by your bed square footage to compare quotes.
  • Bales or rolls? Rolls hold more straw than square bales. Get the count and the type.
  • Longleaf or slash? Longleaf costs more per bale but usually lasts longer before fading. Make sure both quotes use the same straw, or compare the cost per year.
  • Refresh or full install? A light top-up over existing straw takes far fewer bales than a new install on bare soil. Ask what depth they're quoting.
  • Prep work. Is weeding, raking out matted old straw or fresh edging included, or extra?
  • Pre-emergent. Do they put down a granular weed preventer under the straw, and what does it add?
  • Finishing. Are edges tucked, and are the driveway, walks and patio blown off when they leave?
  • Minimums and delivery. Is there a minimum charge for small jobs, and is delivery in the per-bale price or billed separately?
  • Distance from the house. Will they keep straw out of the band right against siding and decks if you ask? (See the Atlanta notes below.)

A quick photo of each bed with rough measurements lets most crews give a firm price without a site visit.

Notes for metro Atlanta homeowners

Pine straw is a homegrown product around Atlanta. Angi notes it's most common in the southern U.S., particularly in Georgia and Florida, and GreenPal says prices tend to sit at the lower end near the pine plantations of Georgia, north Florida and the Carolinas because the straw doesn't travel far. UGA Extension says pine needle-fall is heaviest in fall, winter and early spring, so if you have your own pines, raking your own straw into beds can cut the bill. Fire safety near the house is the main caution. Angi lists pine straw as flammable because dry needles ignite quickly. The NFPA home ignition zone guidance defines the first 5 feet around a home as the immediate zone, recommends keeping that area non-combustible, and says to move mulch, leaves and needles away from exterior walls and keep pine needles out of roofs and gutters. A practical approach is to keep straw out of that band next to siding and decks and use it in island beds, tree rings and outer borders instead. If you're removing old straw, check who hauls it. In the City of Atlanta, the ATL311 yard trimmings page says contractors are responsible for removing yard debris generated from landscaping work under city code Sec. 130-40(c)(4). Residents can set out up to 20 brown yard-trimming bags of no more than 35 pounds each per collection and must schedule a bulk collection for 21 or more. City residents can also drop off yard debris at Hartsfield Landfill, 2175 James Jackson Pkwy. Since UGA recommends topping up old straw rather than removing it, many beds don't need a haul-off at all. Cities and counties outside Atlanta set their own yard-waste rules, so check locally.

Frequently asked questions

How many bales of pine straw do I need?

It depends on the bale size and depth, which is why it's worth asking each installer how many square feet their bales cover. GreenPal says a typical property with 1,000 to 1,500 square feet of beds takes 25 to 40 standard square bales, and suggests adding 10% to 15% for slopes, curved edges and settling.

Does pine straw make my soil too acidic?

Not in any meaningful amount, according to GreenPal, which cites UNH Extension finding that a 2- to 3-inch layer of pine mulch won't shift soil pH measurably. Angi notes the acidity of fresh needles is largely neutralized as they decompose. A soil test is the way to check pH if plants are struggling.

How often should pine straw be replaced?

Usually once or twice a year. Angi says pine straw needs replacing at least once a year, and UGA says it lasts about a year before it starts looking dingy. GreenPal says longleaf straw holds its color 6 to 12 months while slash fades in 4 to 6, so slash beds are often refreshed twice a year.
