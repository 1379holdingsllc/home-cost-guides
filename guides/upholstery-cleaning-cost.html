How Much Does Upholstery Cleaning Cost? 2026 Price Guide

Updated 2026-10-16 · Figures are typical estimates reported by the sources cited at the end of this page.

Upholstered furniture holds onto what carpets do — dust, body oils, pet dander, tracked-in dirt and spills — but it is harder to clean yourself without shrinking fabric, leaving water rings or driving stains deeper into the foam. Professional upholstery cleaning is usually priced per piece, which makes it easier to budget than many other home services, as long as you know how companies define a “sofa” versus a “sectional” and whether fabric protector or stain work is included. This guide pulls together the 2026 ranges published by Angi, HomeGuide and Thumbtack and explains what actually moves a quote.

Quick answer

Angi reports a 2026 national average of about $174 per piece, with most jobs between $120 and $232. By furniture type, Angi lists sofas at roughly $120–$400, sectionals at $200–$500, loveseats at $80–$200, armchairs at $60–$120 and dining chairs at $10–$30. HomeGuide puts a typical service visit around $100–$250, with sofas at $100–$400 and sectionals at $120–$500. Thumbtack reports a national average range of $138–$247, with most people paying around $184, and observed jobs from about $91 on the low end to $382 on the high end. These are typical estimates, not quotes.

How companies price the work

Most cleaners use a flat fee per item. Angi notes some pros also charge about $10–$15 per linear foot or per cushion on large sofas or built-ins, with oversized pieces running roughly 20%–40% above a standard rate, and custom or built-in seating sometimes at $15–$25 per linear foot. HomeGuide flags minimum home-service fees of about $100–$150, which is why cleaning one dining chair alone can feel expensive — you are paying the trip minimum, not just the chair rate. Bundling a sofa, chairs and maybe a carpet cleaning into one visit is usually the way to make the numbers work.

Thumbtack's local examples show how much city-to-city pricing can move: one Sacramento cleaner quoted a lounge chair around $55, a loveseat around $95, a 7-foot couch around $135 and a 10-foot sectional around $190; Portland examples were lower (chair about $40, loveseat $60, couch $80); one Philadelphia example put a standard sofa and loveseat together around $100. Use those as illustrations of spread, not as Atlanta prices.

Fabric and cleaning method

The care tag on the furniture is the first thing a good cleaner checks. Codes generally mean: W (water-based cleaner), S (solvent only), WS (either), or X (vacuum only, no liquid). Ignoring the code is how cushions shrink, dyes bleed and watermarks appear.

HomeGuide groups material pricing similarly: fabric across a wide $20–$400 band depending on size and method, vinyl about $100–$250, and leather about $150–$500. Leather needs dedicated cleaners and conditioners; oils that are wrong for vinyl can make it hard and rough, which is why a carpet cleaner who “also does couches” is not automatically the right call for leather or suede.

By method, Angi lists hot-water extraction (often called steam cleaning) at about $100–$300 per piece, dry foam at $120–$220, and encapsulation at $100–$190. HomeGuide's steam-cleaning band is $20–$300 per item and chemical cleaning $20–$400, again with those trip minimums applying. Hot-water extraction is common on durable synthetics; dry or solvent methods are safer on S-coded and delicate fabrics. Ask which method matches your tag before you compare prices.

What drives the price up

  • Size and shape. A deep sectional with chaise, skirt and tufting takes longer than a plain three-cushion sofa.
  • Soil and stains. Angi lists focused stain or spot treatment around $25–$80; HomeGuide puts extra stain pre-treatment around $30–$70. Pet urine, grease and dye transfers are the usual hard cases.
  • Odors. Angi prices odor/deodorizing add-ons around $15–$35. Smoke and urine often need more than a fragrance spray.
  • Protector. Fabric protector (for example, Scotchgard-type products) runs about $20–$50 on Angi and $20–$100 on HomeGuide; Thumbtack cites roughly $20–$35 for a protective solution. It does not make fabric stain-proof, but it can buy time on future spills.
  • Pet hair and allergens. Angi lists specialized pet-hair or allergen treatment around $15–$40.
  • Access. Angi notes moving or disassembling furniture can add about $20–$50, and same-day or emergency scheduling about $50–$100.

DIY vs. hiring out

Angi puts DIY (rental machine plus supplies) at roughly $40–$100 per session versus professional work around $120–$260 per piece. HomeGuide notes you can handle light spots with household products and that a rented steamer helps on heavier soil — with the caveat that the wrong product or too much water can damage fabric and foam. DIY is reasonable for durable W-coded microfiber you are willing to risk. It is a bad idea on silk, rayon blends that water-spot, antiques, leather you have never conditioned before, or anything with a strong urine odor in the cushion foam. Once liquid soaks into foam, surface cleaning alone often will not fix the smell; a pro with subsurface tools has a better shot, and sometimes the cushion wrap has to be replaced.

Drying time

Angi notes furniture can take anywhere from about 1 to 24 hours to dry, commonly 2–8 hours, depending on fabric, method, piece size, temperature and humidity. Sitting on damp cushions traps moisture in the foam and can lead to mildew or dye transfer. Fans, airflow and a dehumidifier help. In humid climates, plan the cleaning for a day when you can run the AC and keep windows from dumping more moisture into the room.

How often

Angi suggests most households clean upholstery every 12–24 months, and every 6–12 months if there are kids, pets or allergy concerns. HomeGuide suggests about 1–2 times per year for typical use, or 3–4 times per year with kids or pets. Vacuuming with an upholstery tool between visits does more than people expect: it removes the grit that abrades fibers and the dander that washing alone will not fully fix if it has been grinding in for years.

When cleaning is not enough

If the fabric is worn through, the frame is broken, or foam has collapsed, cleaning will not resurrect the piece. HomeGuide lists furniture repair around $100–$500 for chairs and $250–$1,000 for couches, and full reupholstery around $200–$1,500 for chairs and $600–$2,000 for couches. Compare that to replacement before you spend cleaning money on a sofa that is structurally done. Slipcovers HomeGuide prices at about $10–$15 per piece to dry-clean (many fabric ones are machine-washable) are sometimes the cheaper way to protect a piece you clean less often.

How to compare upholstery cleaning quotes

Upholstery bids look simple until two cleaners count pieces differently. Compare:

  • Piece list. Write down each item (sofa, sectional sections, chairs, ottoman). Make sure every bid uses the same list and the same definition of sectional vs. sofa.
  • Care-tag method. Ask which cleaning method they will use for your W, S, WS or X codes. A cheap hot-water price on an S-coded fabric is not a bargain.
  • What's included. Pre-spray, spot treatment, deodorizer, protector and cushion removal are often extras. Get them itemized.
  • Pet urine plan. If odor is the reason you're calling, say so and ask how they treat foam, not just the cover.
  • Minimum trip fee. One chair against a $100+ minimum is a different deal than three pieces in one visit.
  • Drying and satisfaction. Ask how long until you can sit, and what happens if a spot returns as it dries (wicking).

Color-safe testing on a hidden area before full cleaning is a good sign. A cleaner who will not test risky fabric is a risk.

Notes for metro Atlanta homeowners

Humidity is the practical Atlanta wrinkle for upholstery cleaning. Wet fabric and foam dry slowly in a muggy summer, especially in basements and sunrooms without strong AC, and damp cushions are how mildew smells start (see the EPA guide to mold and moisture). Schedule cleaning on a day you can run air conditioning and fans, and avoid packing cushions back under slipcovers until they are fully dry. Spring pollen settles into living-room fabrics the same way it coats cars and window sills; vacuuming with an upholstery tool after peak pollen (check the Atlanta Allergy & Asthma pollen count) stretches the time between professional cleans. Red clay from shoes and pets shows up as orange dust along sofa skirts and ottomans near entryways. Point those areas out when you ask for quotes so the cleaner brings the right spotters instead of promising a uniform result they cannot deliver.

Frequently asked questions

Will cleaning remove pet urine smell from a sofa?

Surface cleaning often is not enough if urine soaked into the foam. Ask about enzyme or subsurface treatment, and be prepared that badly saturated cushions may need foam replacement.

Can I clean leather the same way as fabric?

No. Leather needs dedicated cleaners and conditioners. Water-based carpet tools can stain or dry out leather, so hire someone who regularly cleans leather or follow the maker's care guide.
